# Non-railway transport borrowing, 1750-1948: docks, shipping and the benchmark

**Covers**: dock and harbour companies, coastal shipping and ship mortgages, Bank Rate and bill
rates, the general industrial and commercial benchmark, and the 1930s municipal comparison.
Parent: [cost-of-capital](cost-of-capital.md). Land transport is in
[borrowing-land-transport-1750-1948](borrowing-land-transport-1750-1948.md).

1. The London and India Docks figures are the lowest rates found for any transport borrower in

   1884, both preference (unsecured), at a port with a small and uncertain traffic.

## Assessment

**Did the non-railway transport rate differ from the railway rate?**

Yes, and the direction is consistent. On the only occasion when whole sectors can be compared
on the same measure, 1908, the secured coupons were:

| Sector | Secured coupon in issue, 1908 | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Railways (selected leading companies) | 3.00 to 4.00 | Coupons on 1905 company accounts (railway file F20-F22); yield 3.34 in December 1907 (railway file F10) |
| Docks (London and India) | 3.00 | F35 |
| Canals (Grand Junction, Leeds and Liverpool, Grand Canal) | 3.00 to 4.00 | F14-F16 |
| Docks and harbours (Alexandra, Barry, King's Lynn, Ardrossan) | 4.00 to 4.50 | F36-F41 |
| Shipping (eight companies) | 3.50 to 4.50, mostly 4.50 | F42-F49 |
| Tramways (ten companies) | 3.50 to 5.00, mostly 4.00 to 4.50 | F25-F34 |
| Omnibus (LGOC, Tilling) | 4.00 | F20, F22 |

The reading is that the largest and most statutory transport borrowers — the leading railways,
the London docks and the old canal companies — borrowed at or near the government rate, while the
newer and smaller undertakings in tramways, shipping and omnibus paid one-half to one and a half
points more. The tramway and shipping coupons of 4.00 to 5.00 were below the average rate on new
issues of industrial debentures in 1913 (5.50) and above the railway debenture yield of 3.34 in
1907.

The difference is one of credit and security, not of sector. Within a single company the ladder
runs in the expected order: at London United Tramways 4.00 secured against 5.00 preference
(F30-F31); at Thomas Tilling 4.00 secured against 5.50 preference (F22-F23); at London General
Omnibus 4.00 secured against 5.00 preference (F20-F21); at King's Lynn 4.50 debenture against
4.00 and 5.00 preference of 1869 and 1884 (F38-F40).

**Did the transport borrower pay more than the general industrial borrower?**

The nearest comparison available is the 1908 transport coupons against the 1913 average rate on
new industrial debentures, the closest year in the Bank's series. On that comparison the answer
is no for the secured instrument: tramway and shipping debentures at 4.00 to 4.50 were below the
1913 average of 5.50, and the London docks at 3.00 were far below it. Transport undertakings were
large, asset-backed and often statutory, and the market priced them as such. The higher transport
coupons after 1895 reflect the newness of the electric tramway and steamship companies, not the
transport sector as a class.

**Principal gaps**

1. **No stage coach borrowing rate.** The evidence located shows how coaching was financed
   (coachbuilders owning and leasing the vehicles; innkeepers providing horses from their own
   capital) but states no interest rate. The contemporary sources examined state leasing rates per
   double mile and capital values, not the price of borrowed money.
2. **No road haulage rate and no confirmed hire purchase rate.** No source found states the
   interest charged on hire purchase of lorries or buses in 1919-1948. The material located
   records deposit terms and the existence of hire-purchase schemes, not rates.
3. **Thin coverage 1850-1890.** Between the turnpike returns ending in 1848 and the 1908
   Year-Book there is no continuous non-railway series. Only the isolated canal and dock coupons
   in F14-F16 and F38-F39 fall in or near this period.
4. **No whole-sector average for any non-railway transport sector.** Unlike the Board of Trade
   railway return of 1865 (railway file, F17-F18), no equivalent aggregate was found for canals,
   docks, tramways or shipping. Every figure here is a single company's instrument or a sample.
5. **No bottomry rate.** Bottomry bonds are named in the literature but no rate for a British
   voyage in the nineteenth century was found.
6. **No bank loan or overdraft rate before 1922.** The Bank's corporate borrowing series begins
   in 1922. Canal, dock and shipping companies certainly used banks — the 1908 Year-Book records
   bankers' loans and overdrafts at several companies — but at rates the sources do not state.
7. **Municipal tramway finance is almost absent.** Corporation tramways were financed from the
   rates and by corporation stock, which is a local-authority rate, not a company rate. Only
   F68 records one such rate.
8. **Coupons are far more numerous than yields.** Almost every transport figure here is a coupon
   on an instrument in issue, not a yield observed in the market. Where a price is stated (the
   London and India A debenture stock at 81, the City of Dublin debentures at 95½, Leeds and
   Liverpool debenture stock at 85) the source does not state the yield, and none is computed.

**What the evidence supports for calibration.** A non-railway transport bond should be modelled
as the government rate plus a spread, with the spread set by seniority and by the standing of the
borrower rather than by the sector. The secured coupon for a sound undertaking was 4.00 to 4.50
in 1895-1908, against 3.00 to 4.00 for a railway and 3.00 for the London docks; the unsecured
preference cost 1.00 to 1.50 points more within the same company (computed from F20-F23,
F30-F31); and a small or new undertaking paid 5.00 to 6.00. Before 1850 the ceiling was the usury
maximum of 5.00, and the rate actually paid by turnpike trusts averaged 3.89 over the five years
recorded (computed from F2-F6) because a substantial minority paid nothing.
