# Infrastructure cost evidence

**Covers**: the measured historical figures behind the cost recommendations, by domain, with the
source for each. This is the evidence base; the recommendations and the recorded decisions are in
[infrastructure-cost-synthesis](infrastructure-cost-synthesis.md), and the conversion chain is in
[infrastructure-base-costs](infrastructure-base-costs.md).

Everything here is in 1900 prices unless stated. "Measured" means an actual account, contract or
statutory return; "estimate" means a handbook rate or an author's figure.

The purpose of this document is to preserve the evidence across the multi-domain research
programme, so that when all domains are complete the balance implications can be derived from one
body of figures rather than re-researched.

## 2. Railway permanent way, earthworks and renewal

**Permanent way installed, per km**: L&M 1830 £1,340 (rails £1,740/mile at £12 10s a ton, plus
blocks and sleepers £586/mile); Leicester 1877 £1,790 (47 lb, including labour); Leeds 1881 £1,450
(85 lb, materials only); Dewsbury 1881-82 £1,290 (55 lb). Mills c.1898 materials only: £746 at
65 lb to £1,040 at 90 lb. **Working anchor: £1,300-1,900/km, roughly flat 1830-1900.**

**Formation and minor earthworks**: £110/km (Surrey Iron Railway, 1803), £253/km including fencing
(L&M, 1830), £250-400/km typical (1855-1900). The Midland Counties 1840 heavy earthworks were about
£3,200/km, independently confirming the L&M figure; the £250-400/km band is therefore **bed
preparation**, not bulk earthworks. `[LITERATURE:research-pricing-report-3.md §4]`

**Heavy earthworks**: L&M cuttings and embankments £3,284/km plus Chat Moss £456/km. Grand
Junction contracts 1837-39: 4.4 million cubic yards at about 9d a yard with leads under a mile,
11d at a mile and a half, 1s 1d at three miles. Tring cutting, London & Birmingham: 1.4 million
cubic yards in 2.5 miles, paid 7d to 1s a yard according to depth.

Continuous cutting at 1.5:1 slopes, derived from those rates, per km of route:

| Depth | Single track | Double track |
|---|---|---|
| 5 ft | £440 | £710 |
| 10 ft | £1,390 | £2,020 |
| 20 ft | £4,620 | £6,030 |
| 30 ft | £11,490 | £14,120 |
| 40 ft | £21,620 | £25,610 |
| 60 ft | £58,550 | £66,280 |

**Straight-sided retaining-wall cuttings**, from measured Victorian brickwork and dressed stone
rates: £0.70 per square metre of face at 15 ft, £1.16 at 25 ft, £1.70 at 40 ft.

**Maintenance excluding renewal**: Surrey Iron Railway 1820s £8.6/km/year; North Eastern 1878
£29-60; British Railways 1966 £44; ORR 2015-16 £334. Acworth 1902 (£311) and the 1938 figure
(£384) are **combined maintenance and renewal** and cannot be used as maintenance alone.

**Renewal**: wrought iron 5-25 years depending on traffic, steel 15-25 years, modern 25-45.
Relaying costs 50-60% of new permanent way, because the formation and ballast are reused. NER
relaying 1878 was £661/km against new permanent way at £1,277/km. The 1859 ICE discussion gives
£100 a mile-year with 37 per cent of the cost in rails and 63 per cent in the substructure. The
modern component split (ORR CP5/CP6) is: rail-only renewal 18-22 per cent of a full renewal,
re-ballast 42-46 per cent, rail plus sleeper 43-62 per cent, and full plain-line renewal
£1.41-1.45m per track km in nominal prices. `[LITERATURE:network-rail-track-renewal-unit-costs.md]`

**Urban against rural**: Victorian urban street maintenance ran 5d to 1s 6d a square yard a year
against £62/mile for a rural main road, a factor of about 8-12. Modern urban construction is about
1.5-2 times rural per lane-km.

**Resurfacing** (renewal, not maintenance): DfT 2023 classified roads £28-30/m², a 7 m road about
£1,370/km in 1900 prices, on a cycle of about 13 years.

**Bus stops**: pole, flag and timetable case £300-500; modern aluminium and glass shelter
£7,000-12,000, with lighting about £19,500; real-time display £6,000-24,000.

## 4. Bridges, viaducts and tunnels

**Viaducts**: masonry and brick £55-160/m typical, Ribblehead £833/m as an outlier. Height does
not explain the spread; stone supply, access and foundations dominate.

**Iron**: trestles £48-95/m at 23-63 ft piers, rising to £196-280/m for 150 ft spans and £416/m
for 250 ft spans at 252 ft piers. Humber's worked example: a 1,200 ft bridge at 60 ft with 150 ft
spans, £163/m at 1900 prices, of which the piers are about 25%.

**Tubular and major river**: Conwy £1,321/m; Charing Cross £432/m; Grosvenor £337/m. Major river
crossings are **about 10 times an ordinary underbridge**, range 8-12, which the 1832 London &
Birmingham evidence confirms directly (canal bridge £3,200 against accommodation bridge £300).

**Tunnels**: ordinary double-track bored £45-145/m; 1830s named tunnels £22-113/m; hard ground adds
up to 2-3 times (Kilsby £144/m against Blea Moor £45/m); cut-and-cover in cities £120-170/m; deep
tube per bore £25-61/m in 1890-1900. Mills's 1898 range of £32-£150 a lineal yard is illustrative,
not measured, and he refuses to give an average.

**Maintenance, measured**: Cudworth's 1886 Institution of Civil Engineers paper gives eighteen
years of accounts for two North Eastern viaducts.

| Structure | Upkeep per metre per year |
|---|---|
| Belah viaduct, iron, 195 ft high | £0.25 |
| Deepdale viaduct, iron, 158 ft high | £0.23 |
| Hownes Gill, firebrick | £0.05 |
| Plain line for comparison | £0.19 |

Timber deck renewal is a further £0.52-0.55/m/year and is renewal, not maintenance. **Brick is
about 0.25-0.5 times plain line upkeep, iron 1.2-1.5 times plain line, and timber 7-8 times the
brick rate**; the Cudworth timber renewal itself is 2.7 times plain line, so the 7-8 multiplier
is against brick, not against plain line. [LITERATURE: Cudworth, *Minutes of the Proceedings of
the Institution of Civil Engineers* 85 (1886) 340-342; arithmetic check in research pass 19 §11]

## 5. Canals, docks and locks

**Construction per km**: narrow and barge £1,850-5,560; ship £14,750-33,000; large ship £272,000.

The Leeds & Liverpool's own estimate of one mile of 42 ft broad canal excluding locks, at 1776:
cutting £880, puddling £88, four bridges £1,600, stop-gates £100, fencing £100, towing path £78,
four culverts £240, banks £84, miscellaneous £665, land £640. Earthworks and puddling are about
21% of it; bridges and culverts 45%.

**Maintenance**: £62/km/year routine, from the 1938 comparison of £184/mile for canals against
£1,130 for railways.

**Locks**: narrow £406-427 each, barge £610-850, ship £800-1,000. A useful rate is **£67 per foot
of fall**. Lock gates are renewed on a 20-30 year cycle and that renewal is separate from routine
maintenance.

**Docks and piers per metre**: masonry quay wall £100-131; concrete dock wall £40; rubble harbour
wall £131; timber wharf £18; iron pier £32; heavy stone sea pier £295; Admiralty breakwater £1,061.
A single landing stage of the Margate type, iron, 1,240 ft, cost about £12,000 in 1853.

## 6. Stations, platforms and shelters

**Platform per metre of face, 1900 prices**: timber £2-4, brick-faced £4-8, concrete £5-10, with
precast concrete from the 1930s at the low end.

**Maintenance as a share of capital a year**: timber 4-8%, brick 0.8-1.5%, concrete 1.5-2.5%. The
brick figure is measured (London County Council loan conditions and municipal repair funds);
timber and concrete are estimated from qualitative evidence.

**Canopies and roofs**: £22-47 per square of 9.29 square metres, so a single-sided canopy is
£9-30 per metre of platform face and an overall roof £25-60.

**Shelters**: Brighton Corporation tram shelters, 13 ft by 7 ft glazed timber, £62 plus £7
foundations (1902-16); precast concrete shelter £60 (1953); London Transport standard shelter £150
(1961); Sheringham concrete shelter £200 (1955).

**Station examples**: Low Street, 200 ft platforms and one brick building, £610 (1861); GWR halt,
single wooden building, £261 (1906); Grays, small station building, £33,000 (1954); Southend East
total £50,660 (1932).

## 7. Signals and signalboxes

**One signal post complete, 1912 prices**: 15 ft £16 13s, 20 ft £18 17s, 30 ft £25 2s, 50 ft
£33 7s, including the arm, lamp and wire share to 50 yards from the box. An extra arm on an
existing post £8; a disc £11. Wire beyond that: £12 at 1,000 yards. A complete automatic signal
in 1909 was £97-100 an arm.

**Maintenance**: interlocking and block apparatus cost £27 a lever in 1873 with maintenance at 10%
a year; a lever cost £1.3-1.5 a year to maintain in 1884; an oil signal lamp cost about £3 a year
all in (1885). A whole 1909 mechanical installation was about £28-30 a lever.

**Signalboxes**: a small mechanical box with its signals and instruments about £300 in 1909, plus
£150 a year to man and maintain; Spetisbury, a 9-lever box with eight semaphores and block
instruments, £450 in 1901 with £6.30 a year of apparatus maintenance and no manning; manning three
men per box at £60 a man-year in 1873.

**Signalling per route kilometre**: about £315 in the mechanical era; about £5,200 on the 1959-62
Kent Coast colour-light and power-box scheme, where every running signal cost about £2,272 all in.
That 15-20 fold real increase from mechanical to colour light is the most important fact in this
domain, and it comes from track circuits, cables, interlocking and control rather than from posts.

## 8. Electrification

**Capital, third rail.** Four Southern schemes: LSWR 1913 estimate £4,833 per track km; Guildford
and Dorking 1925 £7,713; Portsmouth No. 1 1937 £7,712; Reading and Camberley 1939 £7,042 (all
nominal). The 1925 and 1937 figures are almost identical in money of the day twelve years apart, so
the real fall came from the price level, not from cheaper work. The 1925 figure includes seven
rotary-converter substations, conductor rail and platform lengthening, and excludes rolling stock.
`[LITERATURE:br-electrification-costs-1955-1991.md]`

**Capital, 25 kV.** The 1955 Modernisation Plan (Cmnd 9691) gives about £125m for main-line fixed
installations: £94,722 per route km if the 820 trunk route miles are meant, £53,200 if all 1,460
plan route miles are. The Southern Region extension is 250 route miles for about £25m (£62,137 per
route km). BTC *Electrification* (1955) gives the only clean 25 kV figure: £38.6m for the
Euston-Manchester-Liverpool power supply and overhead, including clearances, over 495 route miles
(£48,454 per route km; about £24,200 per track km if two tracks). The 1966 West Coast scheme was
£175m for 1,500 track miles including track, signalling and stations (£72,494 per track km, an
upper bound); Hansard (27 May 1968) gives the net capital cost as about £90m including electric
stock. The East Coast scheme was authorised at £306m in 1984 and cost £344.4m at 1983 prices; 60
per cent was infrastructure and 1,400 track miles were electrified, about £91,700 per track km.
`[LITERATURE:br-electrification-costs-1955-1991.md]`

**The earlier £40m for 390 route miles is withdrawn.** 390 miles is the Plan's *suburban*
programme, and the Plan's fixed-installation figure is £125m, not £40m. The suburban programme's
own cost could not be recovered from the scanned table and is recorded as unverified.
`[LITERATURE:br-electrification-costs-1955-1991.md]`

**Fixed equipment.** A 25 kV feeder station is about £1m with about £2m more for the grid
connection (2010). Crossrail's 2014 contract for eight auto-transformer substations plus a feeder
station was £16m, about £1.78m a site. Overhead-line base rates (HS2 cost model, 2011) are £377
per route metre single track, £544 two-track and £1,084 four-track; the Railway Industry
Association's 2019 efficient-electrification figures are £750,000-1,000,000 per single track km,
and the Great Western outturn was about £2m per single track km. Tramway 750 V DC overhead is
£150,000-200,000 per single track km, or about £600,000 per route km with feeder substations and
grid connection. `[LITERATURE:br-electrification-costs-1955-1991.md]`

**Maintenance**: tramway line equipment, maintenance and renewal combined, £58/km a year in 1930-31.
No British electrification maintenance series per track-km-year was found.

**Renewal**: contact wire 35-40 years, catenary with it; registration assemblies 50-60 years;
steelwork 80 years; foundations 120 years. Conductor rail 60-80 years, barely traffic-dependent.

## 9. Airports, power lines and elevated ways

**Runways**: grass £1-2/m²; concrete by strength, £69/m² at PCN 10 rising to £189 at PCN 93;
maintenance 1.6-2.4% of capital a year, overlay every 20-35 years as renewal. Second World War:
444 airfields with paved runways for £200m excluding buildings.

**Terminals**: £10,000-21,000/m² modern; Stansted £1,600/m² in 1991. **Hangars** £250-750/m².
**Control towers** £2.3m (Luton, 48 m) to £50m (Heathrow, 87 m).

**Power lines**: 275 kV lattice £15,535/km in 1959, £1.6m/km in 2015; maintenance about £650/km a
year, overwhelmingly tower painting and vegetation.

**Elevated ways**: DLR City Airport viaduct about £19-24m/km in 2005 prices; the 1978 Queen
Elizabeth II bridge £499,024/km in 1900 prices.

## 10. What each object is meant to represent

Recorded from the forum, largely by the pakset owner, and the basis for every recommendation:

- **Way and forge**: the way is the permanent way on a prepared bed; the forge is the difference
  between renewing on that bed and forging virgin terrain. Halved for a parallel way. Charged again
  on a full rebuild. Charged on a new tunnel but not an upgrade.
- **Way objects** (catenary and similar) are objects laid over a way, distinct from the way itself.
- **Signal objects** represent one post with its arms or lamps, mechanism and share of the wires.
- **Signalboxes** represent the general signalling infrastructure: building, frame, locking, block
  instruments, interlocking, track circuits and control.
- **Platforms**: timber should be cheap to build and dear to maintain, brick dear to build and
  cheap to maintain, concrete between the two but nearer brick.
- **Elevated ways and bridges** should differ from plain way by the real cost differential, not by
  an arbitrary amount.
- **Bridge height and span** are deliberately not costed, because no coherent rule exists.
- **A bridge object bundles the structure and the way on top of it**; separating them is a large
  coding task and has not been attempted.

## 11. Conflicts between the record and the data

These are recorded so that they are resolved deliberately rather than rediscovered later.

1. **Bridges.** A 2022 note records a brick arch viaduct at 23,902c/km against 9,000c/km in game,
   and calls bridges too cheap. The present masonry viaduct is 5,120,000 a tile, nearly 41 million
   a km. Two orders of magnitude separate the two statements and the transition is unexplained.
2. **Station maintenance.** A 2014 note records station buildings at 15-30% of capital a *month*
   against a fair 10% a *year*. The present data is consistent with the inflated figure, not the
   correct one.
3. **Masonry against timber bridges on roads.** The current data gives masonry bridges *higher*
   maintenance than timber, the reverse of the stated intention, and the fix was deferred.
4. **Rail against tram forge.** The rail forge is far too high and the tram forge too low, so the
   differential between the two is too great.
5. **Way renewals** are booked as capital where reality charged them to revenue, which distorts
   every cost-to-revenue ratio used in the earlier calibration.
6. **Depot maintenance** is too *low* (700c against 2,797c) where almost everything else is too
   high.
7. **The canal way object** and the forge have their meanings reversed, as recorded in
   [infrastructure-cost-synthesis](infrastructure-cost-synthesis.md).
