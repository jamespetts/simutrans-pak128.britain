## Assessment

### What is directly usable

Four sources give an account detailed enough to be reduced to a horse-only cost with wages
stated separately. Each conversion below is **[computed]** and shows its inputs.

- **Wandsworth, 1897 (S15).** The most complete single account. Stated total £60 11s 1d per
  horse per year (average 15.13 horses), but that total includes wages, depreciation of the
  horses (£94 4s 6d, or £6 4s 9d per horse), and interest on buildings, harness, the gas
  engine and utensils. Before deducting the £28 19s 3d received for manure, the horse-only
  items (provender, shoeing, veterinary, harness, utensils, stable repairs, rates, insurance,
  gas, water) total **£44 16s 7d per horse**. Adding the wage bill of £114 5s 1d (£7 11s per
  horse) gives **£50 9s** net of the manure credit.
- **Fulham, 1892-93 (S5).** Every component is stated with the average number of horses
  (41½). Food £1,921 13s 1d, farriers' wages £200, nails and shoeing iron £50, forge coal
  £8 13s 6d, harness and utensils £144 8s 6d, veterinary £37 11s 6d. These total
  **£56 18s 5d per horse** **[computed: £2,362 6s 7d divided by 41.5]**. Wages of £308 add
  £7 8s 5d **[computed]**. The fodder quantities are also given by weight and measure, not
  only by cost.
- **Mile End, 1891 (S17).** Complete establishment account, but shoeing and veterinary are
  merged, and the total is given per working day (5s 5d) rather than per horse per year.
- **London General Omnibus Company, 1893 (S3).** Feed 10s a week (£26 a year), shoeing £2 a
  year and veterinary 6s a year, giving **£28 6s** of horse-only cost **[computed: £26 + £2 +
  6s]**. This is the lowest well-attested horse-only figure found. Wages are not given in
  money, only as a headcount of 11,000 men for 22,000 horses across the whole trade.

### Whether the figures combine consistently

They do, within limits, once three distinctions are kept:

1. **Feed only against feed plus bedding.** Paddington (F11) and Camberwell (F6) include
   bedding in the weekly figure. The omnibus company (F13) and the tramway (F10) do not.
2. **Horse-only against establishment cost.** Wandsworth and Mile End include wages,
   depreciation and interest. Horne (S1) includes rent and taxes but not depreciation of
   stables. Cowie (S2) includes depreciation of the horse but not the cost of hay, straw or
   grass, so his £17 17s is not a complete keep figure and must not be treated as one.
3. **Home-grown fodder against purchased fodder.** Cowie charges oats at market price but
   explicitly makes no charge for straw. Barrington charges hay at £3 a ton and oats at the
   year's lowest market rate. Farm figures built this way are lower than town figures for
   that reason, not because farm horses were fed worse.

Tested against each other, the town figures agree closely. Feed per horse per week runs 9s
(tram, 1886), 10s (omnibus, 1893), 14s to 22s 2d (Newcastle, 1882), 16s 3d (Paddington,
1892), 17s 9½d (Fulham, 1892-93) and 24s (Camberwell, 1876, including bedding and at retail
tender prices). Shoeing runs from 8d a week (North Metropolitan, 1886) to 1s 9d a week
(Norfolk, 1919), with the London General at 9.2d a week **[computed: £2 divided by 52]**.
Veterinary runs from 6s a year (London General, 1893) to about £1 (Wandsworth, 1897). The
ratios between components are stable enough to be treated as one cost structure.

| London coal cart horse, 1830 (20s a week) | £52 |
| Newcastle Corporation, 1882 (22s 2d a week, before reorganisation) | £57 12s 8d |
| Camberwell stable horse, 1876 (24s a week, including bedding, retail prices) | £62 8s |

**Horse only, excluding the wages of the man and all capital charges.** Four complete
accounts give:

| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| London General Omnibus Company, 1893 | £28 6s |
| Wandsworth District Board of Works, 1897 | £44 16s 7d |
| Fulham Vestry, 1892-93 | £56 18s 5d |
| London coal wharf, 1830 (keep, farrier and harness; rent, wages and depreciation excluded) | £59 13s 6d |

**Including wages, excluding depreciation and interest:** £50 9s (Wandsworth, 1897, net of
the manure credit).

**Including wages, depreciation and interest, as the sources state the total:** £60 11s 1d
(Wandsworth, 1897); £79 13s (Norfolk, 1919, outside the period).

The single most defensible statement is this: **between 1830 and 1900 the keep of a working
horse in Britain, covering feed, farriery, veterinary attention, stabling, bedding and
harness but excluding the wages of the man and the depreciation of the animal, falls between
about £28 and £60 a year.** The four complete accounts sit at £28 6s, £44 16s 7d, £56 18s 5d
and £59 13s 6d, so the middle of the range is around £45.

Two qualifications govern the range. First, the low anchor is a single very large stud
(10,000 horses) buying forage wholesale in 1893; a small establishment could not buy at that
price. Second, the upper anchors include London retail-priced fodder, and 1830 prices before
the fall in grain prices after 1846 and again after 1873. A provincial stud in the 1880s and
1890s is most likely to sit near £30 to £40; a London vestry stable near £45 to £57.

### What the evidence does not supply

- **No railway company horse account was found.** Shunting and cartage horses kept by
  railways are the closest comparator for freight horses and remain unresearched.
- **No canal company horse account was found.** The 1843 Institution of Mechanical Engineers
  discussion gives cost of horse labour per ton of coal raised, not keep per horse.
- **No mail coach contract cost per horse was found.** The 1841 return of coaches carrying
  letters gives rates per double mile paid to horsing contractors, not the contractors' own
  costs, and the PDF could not be read (HTTP 403).
- **Veterinary costs are thin before 1890.** Cowie's 5s allowance (1845) is the only earlier
  figure found, and it is an allowance, not an account.
- **Stabling is rarely separated** from the rent of the whole premises. Only Fulham,
  Wandsworth and Mile End separate it at all.
- **Nothing was found for 1750-1760 other than the 1760 coach account,** and nothing at all
  between 1760 and 1827.

### Note on the pakset's current values

The pakset carries "estimate 2 per month, 1.5 for pony" and "estimate .01 per kilometre",
both unsourced. The figures above are in pounds per horse per year and cannot be mapped onto
"2 per month" without knowing the unit that value is expressed in and the price index that
applies to the year in question. What can be said is that £2 a month is £24 a year
**[computed]**, which is below every horse-only figure found here and less than the feed
alone of a London omnibus horse in 1893. If "2 per month" is meant in pounds it is too low;
if it is in index units, the conversion has to be done before any comparison is possible.
This is recorded as an open question, not as a finding.
