# Road vehicle maintenance, 1959-2016

**Covers**: published road vehicle maintenance costs per mile after 1958, chiefly the Commercial
Motor Tables of Operating Costs, with the scope and mileage base of each figure. Parent:
[vehicle-cost-evidence](../vehicle-cost-evidence.md). The aircraft figures are in
[aircraft-maintenance](aircraft-maintenance.md).

## Road vehicles, 1959-2016

All figures in this section come from the Commercial Motor Tables of Operating Costs or from
sources that reproduce them, except where stated. The Tables divide total operating cost into
ten items: standing costs of licences, wages, rent and rates, insurance, interest; and running
costs of fuel, lubricants, tyres, maintenance and depreciation. Every figure below is the
**maintenance** item alone unless the row says otherwise. Maintenance in the Tables is defined
by the journal as "expenditure on any work necessary to keep the vehicle in a clean, efficient
and roadworthy condition. This item of cost therefore includes washing, servicing and major
repairs" (Commercial Motor, 16 December 1966).

**Note on the 1974 figure.** At 1.48p per mile it is less than half the 1973 Tables average of
3.36p, for a heavier vehicle. The most likely explanation is that the answer treats maintenance
as a time-related or workshop-overhead item, since the same answer adds "a reasonable allocation
... for overheads such as heating, lighting, cost of maintenance facility if provided ... It is
suggested that 20 per cent would not be inappropriate". The figure should not be used as a
like-for-like continuation of the Tables series.

## Assessment

### Figures that are directly usable

1. **BOAC engineering cost per flying hour, 1969/70 and 1972/73, four types each.** These are
   the only British money-per-flying-hour maintenance figures found for any period before 2000.
   They are usable as a *type-level, long-haul* anchor for 1969-73. Two limits: "engineering" is
   undefined in the source, and the compiler warns that the figures do not reconcile exactly with
   BOAC's annual reports.
2. **The Commercial Motor goods vehicle maintenance series, 1963-1970.** Twelve vehicle/mileage
   observations with the maintenance item isolated from fuel, lubricants, tyres and depreciation
   and with the assumed weekly mileage stated every time. This is the strongest road-goods
   evidence in the file and the only part of the road series that is internally consistent over
   time.
3. **The Commercial Motor coach figures, 1964, 1969, 1970.** Three observations on the same
   basis as the goods vehicle series, and the 1964 pair gives a petrol and a diesel coach in the
   same article at the same date.
4. **Merseyside PTE 1982/83.** "Repairs and maintenance 53.7p per mile" is an undertaking's own
   cost head, with traffic operations, management and asset utilisation separated. It is the
   single cleanest bus maintenance figure in the file.
5. **FTA 2016.** Maintenance separated from fuel and tyres, with annual mileage stated, and
   split between tractor and trailer for the articulated vehicle.

### Usable with a caveat

- **Aircraft Commerce A320 budget (c.2006).** Internally consistent and itemised by check type,
  but it is a benchmark built from MRO and operator data, not an achieved operator cost, and it
  is in United States dollars at an assumed $70 per man-hour labour rate. It should never be
  presented as "what an A320 costs to maintain".
- **IATA MCX FY2024.** A genuine multi-airline measured average, but worldwide and de-identified,
  with no British carrier identified and no statement of how many of the 28 airlines are British.
- **BA "Engineering and other aircraft costs".** A published audited line, but its scope is wider
  than maintenance (the label says "and other aircraft costs") and no denominator in hours is
  published, so it cannot be turned into a per-hour figure from published data alone.
- **BUA 1968/69.** A company's own figure for its engineering department, with labour and
  materials separated, but with no fleet size, no aircraft count and no hours. It cannot be
  converted to a per-hour figure from the source.
- **1981 Hansard figures (9d. and 10d. per road mile).** Official, but they bundle policing with
  maintenance and are per road mile for a class of lorry, not an operator's accounts.
- **1974 Volvo F88 (1.48p per mile).** A journal costing from operator-supplied data that appears
  to treat maintenance partly as overhead; not comparable with the Tables series.

### Can a British operator cost per hour be built for any period?

- **1969-1973: yes, for BOAC, at type level.** The engineering column of the Hall/BOAC breakdown
  gives £102 to £154 per flying hour across four types in two years. This is the only period for
  which a British per-hour maintenance figure was obtained.
- **1956-1968: partly, but not in money per hour.** For BEA the evidence is a share of direct
  operating cost (25-30 per cent, 1956), utilisation in hours per aircraft per year (1957-58),
  and check and overhaul intervals. For BUA it is an annual department cost with no hours. A
  per-hour figure for these years would require a derived ratio, which this file does not make.
- **1974-2024: no.** No British operator publishes maintenance per flying hour in the sources
  found. BA publishes one aggregate line; the CAA publishes item 17 maintenance and overhaul in
  pounds sterling by airline, but the pairing of that item with a published hours denominator was
  not achieved within this search. Power-by-the-hour contract rates are not published by any
  provider found.
- **The pre-1956 gap is not closed.** The brief asked for 1930 onward. Nothing between 1930 and
  1949 was found beyond the 1949 BEA overhaul-life and hourly-purchase arrangements already in
  `vehicle-maintenance-sources.md`, and those carry no money figure.

### How far the road vehicle series can be carried

- **Goods vehicles: 1950-1974 with gaps, then a break.** With the earlier file, the series covers
  1950, 1952, 1953, 1955, 1958, 1963, 1965, 1966, 1967, 1968, 1969, 1970, 1973 and 1974. After
  1974 the money-per-mile maintenance item was not recovered for any year until 2016. The 1981
  figure is a different quantity (maintenance plus policing), the 1989 figure is a five-year
  contract total without a per-mile rate, and the 1995 figure is a percentage movement rather
  than a level.
- **Buses and coaches: 1948-1983 with gaps, nothing after 1983.** The bus/coach evidence is 1948,
  1955 (Chambers report, four undertakings), 1964 (two coaches), 1969, 1970 (coach) and 1982/83
  (Merseyside PTE). No bus maintenance figure per mile was found for the 1990s or 2000s.
- **The 1980s and 1990s are the weakest part of both parts of the brief.** For road goods
  vehicles the Tables of Operating Costs certainly continued to publish a maintenance figure in
  every edition through those decades, but the digitised archive presents those tables as page
  images with no machine-readable text, and the journal's articles that quote the tables in
  running text give totals, tyres or fuel rather than the maintenance item. Recovering that
  stretch requires reading the printed editions (the 1986/87, 1992/93 and 1995/96 volumes) or the
  RHA/DFF *Goods Vehicle Operating Costs* volumes, neither of which is available as text online.

### Definitional traps found in this material

1. **"Engineering" at BOAC is not "maintenance".** It is one column of a six-column operating
   cost breakdown; the source does not say whether it contains engineering overhead, and it
   certainly excludes depreciation (shown as "obsolescence") and fuel.
2. **The A320 benchmark is a budget, not a cost.** Every figure in A6 is a planning allowance
   built from assumed man-hour rates, assumed utilisation and assumed non-routine ratios. The
   source itself reports a variation of $160-240 per FH across the A319, A320 and A310, mainly
   from engine-related cost, and a fall of about $110 per FH when the average flight cycle time
   rises from 1.5 to 2.0 FH.
3. **IATA's $1,522 per flight hour is a cross-fleet average.** It mixes widebodies, narrowbodies
   and regional aircraft, and the report states the data is un-normalised. It is not a figure for
   any one aircraft type.
4. **"Maintenance and policing" is not maintenance.** The 1981 figure of 9d. per road mile is
   the only 1980s goods-vehicle per-mile figure obtained, and it is not comparable with the
   Tables series.
5. **The Commercial Motor maintenance item excludes tyres and depreciation by construction.**
   Both are separate running-cost items in the same table. The Merseyside PTE head "repairs and
   maintenance" does not state whether tyres are inside it, and bus undertakings commonly put
   tyres in a separate account.
6. **The 1989 contract maintenance quotes exclude glass and tyres**, and one manufacturer's quote
   (Renault) excluded the inspections required for O-licensing until the journal added them. Any
   comparison of those quotes is therefore not like-for-like even in the source's own view.
7. **A 25-30 per cent share of direct operating cost is not a cost per hour.** The BEA 1956
   figure moves with fuel price and crew cost as well as with maintenance productivity, so it
   cannot be used to track maintenance over time.

### Open questions

- Whether the CAA's item 17 "Maintenance and overhaul" can be paired with a published aircraft-hours
  denominator for 1991-1998, which would give a British airline per-hour series for that period.
  The CAA's *UK Airlines* tables carry both the expense item and traffic data, but the page-level
  PDF extraction obtained did not join them.
- Whether the printed Commercial Motor Tables of Operating Costs for 1975-1999 can be consulted
  directly, which would close the 1974-2016 gap in the goods vehicle series.
- Whether any British charter or freight operator (Britannia, Monarch, Dan-Air, Tradewinds,
  Air Bridge Carriers) published engineering cost per hour in an annual report or in the trade
  press. Dan-Air Engineering's sale price (£27.5 million, 1991), employee count (1,600) and
  capacity exceeding its sister airline's requirements by 45 per cent are recorded in secondary
  accounts, but no operating cost per hour was found.
