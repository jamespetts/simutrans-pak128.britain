# Vehicle maintenance and running cost: published historical figures

**Covers**: published maintenance and running costs of British railway, road, sea and air
vehicles, with the inclusion and exclusion of each figure stated. Parent:
[vehicle-cost-evidence](../vehicle-cost-evidence.md).

Compiled 2026-11-16. Evidence only; no recommendation, no conversion, no deflation, no
estimation. Every figure below is recorded as the source states it, in the source's own unit.
Where a per-mile or per-vehicle figure is given, the mileage or fleet base is recorded if the
source states it. The "Includes / excludes" column is the most important one and is reproduced
from the source's own wording wherever possible.

Abbreviations: d. = pence (pre-decimal unless stated); s. = shillings; mpw = miles per week;
P/S = primary or secondary.

**Definitional note from the primary source.** Fisher's *Railway Accounts and Finance* reproduces
the pre-1911 company form for "Repairs and Renewals of Locomotives": wages of mechanics for
repairs, materials for repairs, salaries, stationery and printing, repairs of shops and engine
turntables, gas. It reproduces the post-1911 form with **separate** subheads "Complete renewals
(wages, materials)", "Repairs and partial renewals (wages, materials)", "Purchase of new
locomotives", "Workshop expenses" and "Repair and renewals of machinery and plant". It also
records that the split between "Carriages" and "Wagons" in the carriage and wagon account was
"made half-yearly" by the superintendent, not continuously. (J. Alfred Fisher, *Railway Accounts
and Finance*, 4th edn, London, c.1911; preface treats the 1911 Act as a Bill not yet passed.
https://archive.org/stream/accountsfrailway00fishrich/accountsfrailway00fishrich_djvu.txt)

## Assessment

### Directly usable

- **Hill 1895 (locomotives, carriages):** the only source found that gives repair-and-renewal
  cost per train-mile *and* per engine-year *and* per vehicle-year for the same nineteen
  companies over the same five years, with an explicit statement that "maintenance" means
  repairs **plus renewals** and excludes running expenses. The average of 2.51d. per train-mile
  and the per-engine range of £184 to £253 are the strongest nineteenth-century anchors found.
- **TRB Special Report 180 (British Railways diesel and electric):** total maintenance cost per
  locomotive-year and per km, with annual distance stated, for six locomotive classes. The
  cleanest twentieth-century per-vehicle-year figures obtained.
- **Landau 2004 boiler table:** usable only as *boiler* maintenance per mile, but it shows the
  spread between classes (0.80d. to 4.89d. per mile) and the average (1.36d.), which is directly
  relevant to how much of a locomotive's repair bill is boiler work.
- **Commercial Motor lorry figures 1950-1958:** maintenance stated separately from fuel,
  lubricants, tyres, depreciation and standing charges, with the assumed weekly mileage given
  every time. The best-structured road-goods evidence found.
- **Keighley 1927-28 trolleybus:** a per-vehicle-mile repair and maintenance figure with the
  vehicle-mile denominator published (308,578), so it can be checked.

### Usable with a caveat

- **Chambers report bus figures (1955):** a consistent cross-undertaking comparison, but the
  quoted passage does not define what is inside "maintenance". Treat as a comparative index, not
  as a definable cost.
- **Hill's per-company figures:** the NER figure (4.40d.) is distorted by the company's practice
  of building locomotives to revenue and later transferring them to capital; the author says so.
  The NBR figure (1.47d.) is unexplained by the author.
- **Landau boiler figures:** compiled at second hand from Cox and Cook, with an unexplained
  County class outlier and an uncertain administration-cost element.
- **TRB dollar figures:** converted to US dollars by the source; the conversion basis and year
  are not stated in the extract.
- **Ship figures:** Martin's 5% is an estimate; the MIT 1947 figures are costings with an
  explicit reserve element; the NBER 40% groups repairs with interest and depreciation.
- **Aircraft:** the only genuine per-hour money figures found are US Form 41 averages for the
  A320. The historical British figures are overhaul lives and man-hours, not money.

### Main definitional traps found

1. **"Repairs and renewals" is one account, not two, in most nineteenth-century British company
   accounts.** Hill states plainly: "the more general rule appears to be to include the cost of
   renewals with that of repairs", and he therefore substitutes the single word "Maintenance".
   Any pre-1911 per-mile "repair" figure probably contains whole replacement locomotives.
2. **The 1911 Act form splits the same money three ways.** Fisher reproduces "Complete renewals
   (wages, materials)", "Repairs and partial renewals (wages, materials)" and "Purchase of new
   locomotives" as separate subheads under one abstract. A series that spans 1911 can step for
   accounting reasons alone.
3. **Running expenses are a different abstract from maintenance.** Fuel, water, enginemen's and
   firemen's wages, cleaners, shed wages and stores sit in "Locomotive Running Expenses"
   (Abstract C); repairs and renewals sit in "Maintenance and Renewal of Rolling Stock"
   (Abstract B). Figures that combine them (for example the LNER 26.47% of receipts) cannot be
   used interchangeably with per-mile repair figures.
4. **Train-mile and engine-mile are not interchangeable.** Hill notes that shunting is included
   in engine mileage at between four and eight miles an hour, and that companies with low annual
   mileage per engine show the highest cost per train-mile. A per-train-mile figure embeds the
   engine-to-train ratio.
5. **Commercial Motor's maintenance is two subheads, not one.** Maintenance (d) is "routine
   operations, such as washing, greasing, repainting and varnishing"; maintenance (e) is "repairs
   and overhauls". Some articles quote (d) and (e) together (1.89d. for the 1953 6-tonner), others
   quote a single "maintenance" line from the Tables. The two are not the same quantity.
6. **Tyres are outside maintenance in the Commercial Motor system but inside some other bus
   figures.** The Tables list fuel, lubricants, tyres, maintenance and depreciation as five
   separate running costs. Any bus "maintenance" figure of unknown provenance may or may not
   contain tyres.
7. **Depreciation is priced by mileage, not by time, in the road vehicle tables.** The 1954
   article states that depreciation is assessed by mileage, with a 100,000-mile life for
   low-priced and 200,000-240,000-mile life for high-priced vehicles, an allowance for residual
   value, and an obsolescence allowance where annual mileage is below 24,000 (mass-produced) or
   48,000 (individually manufactured). Maintenance and depreciation therefore move in opposite
   directions with utilisation.
8. **Ship "maintenance" may be a reserve.** The MIT 1947 schedules explicitly include a "repair
   reserve" alongside current repairs and annual overhaul, so the annual charge is a provision
   rather than money spent.
9. **Aircraft maintenance in the CAA definition includes overhead.** Item 17 includes engineering
   base costs, training, travel, accommodation and office costs, so it is not a direct hourly
   labour-and-materials rate. The man-hours-per-flight-hour figures are labour only.
10. **Percentages of receipts and of operating cost are not percentages of capital.** The LNER
    26.47% includes fuel and wages; the TU Delft 40% is a share of operational cost; only
    Martin's 5% and Webb's one-quarter to one-third are statements about capital or first cost.
11. **No deflation or conversion has been applied anywhere in this file.** Figures in dollars,
    in pre-decimal pence, in shillings and in "1954 prices" appear exactly as the sources state
    them, and are not comparable with one another without work that is outside this brief.

### Gaps

- No **per-wagon-per-year** figure for the 20th century. Hill's Table III (read from page images
  2026-11-24) closes the 19th; the pre-1911 published returns carry no wagon-only money line at
  all (settled 2026-11-24 — a combined "carriages and wagons" head only), and the post-1911
  Ministry of Transport returns do separate wagons but are HathiTrust/print only. No measured
  wagon overhaul price exists in any era; the surviving route is archival repair invoices
  (Oxfordshire B8/A5, Essex D/F 27/2/108, Chasewater RCH), none digitised.
- No **British locomotive repair cost per mile for 1900-1950** was obtained from a primary
  source. The gap between Hill (to 1892) and the BR boiler table (1954) is real and should not be
  bridged by interpolation.
- No **aircraft maintenance cost per hour for a British operator before 1970** was obtained.
- Ship evidence is the weakest of the seven sections: one estimate of maintenance as a share of
  capital, one docking cost, one dry-dock rent and two 1947 costings.
