**The account line itself.** Fisher's *Railway Accounts and Finance* reproduces the statutory
forms. The pre-1911 form carries "Repairs and Renewals of Carriages and Wagons" with wages,
materials, salaries, stationery and workshop repairs; the post-1911 form separates "Complete
renewals (wages, materials)", "Repairs and partial renewals (wages, materials)", "Purchase of new
vehicles" and "Workshop expenses". Fisher also records that the split between "Carriages" and
"Wagons" within the combined account was "made half-yearly" by the superintendent, not
continuously. (J. Alfred Fisher, *Railway Accounts and Finance*, 4th edn, London, c.1911.
https://archive.org/stream/accountsfrailway00fishrich/accountsfrailway00fishrich_djvu.txt)

The consequence for a per-wagon-year figure is stated plainly: **before 1911 the line includes
whole replacement wagons; after 1911 it does not.**

### 3. Hauled coaches

The same Beeching table gives the coach level: £55.0m for 46,089 multiple-unit cars, carriages
and parcels vans = £1,193 a carrying vehicle-year, and £1,012 a coach-year with the MU cars
removed at 1.75 times a coach. In 1900 money that is £183, against Hill's £34.3 gross (£22.3 net
of renewals) in 1892. Research pass 43 extends the series: £374-686 a coach-year in 1984 (derived
from the HST £2.28 a train-mile rate), £308-615 in 2013 (RDG EMU rate ÷ 3 for a coach) and
£296-433 in 2024-25 (ORR medians ÷ 3). The recommended ladder is 33 / 140 / 265 / 460 / 440 GBP
(1900 money) at 1900 / 1950 / 1970 / 1990 / 2020; pass 45 applies it, and pass 47 splits it into
a continuous head and works-overhaul events of 28 / 24 / 19 / 12 / 10 / 8 per cent of the annual
total by era ([research-pricing-report-44](../cost-balancing/research-pricing-report-44.md)).
The
wagon:carriage ratio falls from 12.6 per cent in 1892 to 5.4 per cent in 1961 and is held at 5.4
per cent after; scaling wagons by the carriage index alone overstates the 1961 wagon by 2.35
times, so the ratio must be allowed to decline. Evidence, method and error bars:
[research-pricing-report-43](../cost-balancing/research-pricing-report-43.md).

## Scheduled against unscheduled maintenance

**No source was located that states a money split between scheduled and unscheduled maintenance
for British railway vehicles, for any period.** What exists is of four kinds.

**1. A scope hierarchy that separates light scheduled work from heavy periodic work.**
London Underground's benchmark counts maintenance levels 1, 2 and 3 and excludes level 4
("programme lift") and levels 5, 6 and 7 ("heavy overhauls"). That is a split by scope and
periodicity, not by cause: a defect found during a level 2 exam is inside the included figure, and
the same defect found in traffic is also inside it. (TfL, Item 10, 29 June 2011, para 194.)

**2. Scheduled intervals, stated without cost.** Denmark, DSB S-tog: a standard check every 50,000
km (about 4 hours), minor overhauls every 100,000 km or 150,000 km by unit type (about 2 days),
major overhauls every 600,000 km; unscheduled work graded into failure categories A, B, B1, C, C1
and D with remedy deadlines from immediate to "when the unit next comes in". No cost is attached
to any of them. (DTU, *Rolling Stock Planning at DSB S-tog: Processes, Cost Structures and
Requirements*, Technical Report. https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/117698091/Rolling_Stock_Planning.pdf)

**3. Failure frequency, which is the driver of unscheduled work.** Class 66: 180 days mean time
between failures targeted, against 16 days for a Class 47 (Railway Gazette International, 1998).
Peer-group MDBF: good-practice EMU twice the average; DMU cars half as good as EMU cars (Railway
Gazette International, 2006). London Underground: car MDBF improved 157% (BCV), 173% (SSL) and
216% (Tube Lines) over eight years to 2010/11 (TfL, Item 10, Appendix A para 183).

**4. Repair triggers that mix time, distance and output.** Freightliner times Class 66 heavy
overhauls on "a combination of mileage, elapsed time, and megawatt output". GB Railfreight's Class
73 wheelset life is six years on Southern and three years in Scotland, on the same locomotive type.
London Underground's Piccadilly line regime "is based on hours operated and not calendar days".

**The negative finding matters for the model.** A pakset that charges a single per-vehicle-mile
maintenance rate is charging a quantity that the industry itself does not measure that way: the
published British figures are either total cost per vehicle-mile (RDG, Civity, ScotRail) or total
cost per vehicle-year (London Underground). The scheduled and unscheduled components are separated
in maintenance *planning* documents by interval and scope, and in *accounts* not at all.

## Definitional traps found in this material

1. **"Fully normalised" is not outturn cost.** The Civity figures are normalised for cars per
   train and partly for annual running performance, and PPP-normalised across countries. They
   compare operators; they are not what any operator spent.
2. **"Rolling stock charges" in ORR statistics is lease plus maintenance.** It cannot be used as a
   maintenance figure, and ORR says so.
3. **A lease rate is not a maintenance rate.** RDG prints £1.43 and £0.97 per vehicle mile for
   capital lease against £0.80 and £0.44 for maintenance. The lease number is the larger of the
   two and is the one most often quoted as "the cost of running a train".
4. **London Underground's Northern line figure contains heavy maintenance that the other lines
   exclude.** The report says so; a cross-line comparison that ignores it is wrong.
5. **The Victoria line's new fleet is a contract price (TSSSA), not a cost of work done.** It is
   excluded from the benchmark for that reason.
6. **The pre-1911 wagon account includes complete renewals.** A series that crosses 1911 steps for
   accounting reasons alone, before any change in real maintenance.
7. **Per train-km and per vehicle-mile differ by the cars per train.** RDG's figures are per
   vehicle mile; Civity's are per train-km; the London figures are per car-year. None converts to
   another without a train formation and a mileage, neither of which is in the same table.
8. **Design or contract figures are not outturn averages.** The £200,000 Class 66 rebuild is the
   cost of one event as specified at order in 1998; the £4.7 million Class 99 is a purchase price.
9. **"Provision" in the Civity definition** ("maintenance ... material, cleaning, provision") is
   not expanded anywhere in the report; it most likely means the provision of spares or of
   maintenance capacity, but the report does not say.
10. **Wagon repair is not one trade.** The Midland's 1919 return separates home, common-user and
    private-owner wagons because the party paying differs. A per-wagon figure for one category is
    not a figure for the fleet.

## Assessment

### Directly usable

1. **RDG, £0.80 diesel and £0.44 electric per vehicle mile (February 2013 prices).** The single
   most usable modern pair: same methodology, same price base, same denominator, and the source
   states both the derivation (merged anonymised TOC and ROSCO data) and the scope (total
   maintenance, excluding lease, energy and track). Two figures that pin the diesel-to-electric
   ratio at 1.8:1 in maintenance.
2. **Civity, nine GB figures per train-km by sector.** The only source that gives a *range* as well
   as an average, which is what a pakset needs if it is not to make every operator identical. Must
   be used as a normalised benchmark, never as outturn spend.
3. **London Underground, per car-year, 2009/10.** Twelve figures with a scope statement that names
   the maintenance levels included and excluded. This is the only British per-vehicle-year figure
   located with a defensible boundary, and it is the right shape for the engine's fixed cost.
4. **Railway Gazette 1998, Class 66 and Class 47 heavy rebuild costs and intervals.** Two
   per-event costs with an interval attached, which is what a periodic overhaul charge needs.
5. **Civity and Railway Gazette 2006 ratios** (DMU twice EMU; traction twice coaches; good practice
   40% below average). Ratios survive price-base uncertainty better than levels do.

### Usable with a caveat

- **ScotRail's £1.36 and £0.80 per vehicle mile.** The totals are corroborated by *Rail Engineer*;
  the component split rests on a forum transcription of a lecture. Use the DMU:EMU ratio (1.7:1,
  consistent with RDG's 1.8:1) with more confidence than the levels.
- **Arup's £0.4bn whole-industry maintenance for 2009/10.** Sound as an aggregate, but it is a
  modelled allocation from DfT data, and Arup itself records "a degree of uncertainty in relation
  to their accuracy and granularity".
- **The Midland Railway repair charges (2s 6d minimum; 9d at the GNR).** Genuine per-transaction
  prices, but a transaction is not a year, and the transcription is second-hand.
- **The 1919 Midland throughput and the nine-month repair interval.** Good evidence of how often a
  wagon was shopped; no money attached.

### Not usable as maintenance

- ORR's £2,450m "rolling stock charges" (lease plus maintenance).
- RDG's £1.43 and £0.97 capital lease figures.
- The £9 17s 6d per wagon per annum Birmingham deferred-payment instalment.
- The RCH 1s per wagon per year commuted charge.
- The £4.7m Class 99 and the £375m Class 66 order.

### The scheduled / unscheduled question

**No money split was found.** This is the honest answer to the most important part of the brief.
The best available substitutes, in order of usefulness, are:

1. **London Underground's level hierarchy** (1-3 included; 4 and 5-7 excluded), which is the only
   British source that puts a boundary round "routine scheduled maintenance" and states what falls
   outside it. If the model needs a split, this is the definition to adopt, because it is an
   operator's own accounting boundary rather than an analyst's invention.
2. **Interval data**: Class 66 at 1.6 million km between rebuilds; DSB at 50,000 / 100,000 / 600,000
   km; Midland wagons at nine months; Class 73 wheelsets at three or six years.
3. **Failure-frequency data** (MTBF 180 days against 16 days; MDBF by fleet), which is the only
   quantitative proxy for the size of the unscheduled tranche.

The practical route to a split, if one is wanted, is to combine (1) with (2): charge the scheduled
tranche from the level 1-3 rate per car-year and the unscheduled tranche from a failure rate times
a repair cost. Neither input is published for British main-line vehicles. Anyone who quotes a
percentage split for UK traction is quoting an assumption, not a measurement.

### The per-wagon-year figure: solved 2026-11-24

**Hill 1895, Appendix II Table III** ("Cost of wagon maintenance per vehicle", pp. 242-243; note
that Table I is locomotives and Table II is carriages) gives the figure for nineteen companies,
five years ending June 1892, covering "maintenance, that is the cost of repairs and renewals":
**average £4 4s. per wagon-year**, range £2 4s. (L&NWR, 55,126 mostly-mineral wagons) to £6 10s.
(LCDR, 2,045 wagons). Low-utilisation large fleets (Caledonian, NBR, L&NWR) sit at £2-2 12s.;
small intensively-worked fleets (LCDR, LSWR) at £6-6 10s. Read from the page images of Internet
Archive `minutesproceedi43britgoog`, 2026-11-24; the full attributed table is in
[published-maintenance](published-maintenance.md) §2. The GER half-yearly series (Table IV) gives
wages and materials separately. The 20th-century substitute is a 1929 private-owner repair
contract at £5 per wagon-year (Essex Archives D/F 27/2/108).

The routes, as now settled:

1. **Railway Returns, "repairs and renewals of wagons" divided by wagons in stock.** Settled
   2026-11-24: the pre-1911 published returns carry no wagon-only money line at all — only a
   combined "Repairs and Renewals of Carriages and Waggons" — so this is a structural absence,
   not an OCR problem. The post-1911 Ministry of Transport returns do separate "Complete renewals
   (wagons)" and "Repairs and partial renewals (wagons)", so a 1912+ series is realistic from
   those volumes, but they are HathiTrust/print only.
2. **Hill 1895, Appendix II Table III** — solved, see above.
3. **The private-owner repair contract.** Gloucester Railway Carriage & Wagon sold private-owner
   wagons on seven-year deferred payment with a repair contract attached, and Wagon Repairs Ltd
   took over such contracts from 1918 (for example 482 ICI wagons transferred from Gloucester in
   1934-36). One measured rate exists (Colchester Gas Company 1929, £5 per wagon-year, Essex
   Archives D/F 27/2/108). Further rates are in undigitised archives: Gloucester RCW, Charles
   Roberts, TNA RAIL, Oxfordshire History Centre B8/A5, and the Railway Clearing House papers at
   Chasewater ("Private Wagons Stopped for Repairs" 1926; "Re. Repairs to Wagons" 1943) — a
   reading-room or archive-enquiry route. No measured wagon overhaul price exists in any era.
4. **Board of Trade and Railway Clearing House returns.** The RCH administered the pooling of
   repair work and the commuted charges; its published schedules give prices per job rather than
   per year, so they are a means of building a per-year figure bottom-up from a repair frequency,
   not a per-year figure in themselves.
5. **For the modern period, nothing public exists at wagon level.** The operators let full-service
   maintenance contracts (GB Railfreight, as reported in 2024) and publish neither the price nor
   the wagon count it covers. The only route is a lessor's or maintainer's contract, which is
   commercial.

### Gaps

- **No British multiple-unit maintenance figure for 1960-1994.** The sector benchmarks begin with
  Civity (2009 data). The Transportation Research Board's *Special Report 180* carries British
