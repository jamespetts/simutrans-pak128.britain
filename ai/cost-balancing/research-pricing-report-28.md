# British horse-drawn road passenger vehicles: purchase and repair costs, 1750-1903

Research pass for Pak128.Britain-Ex cost balancing. Compiled 23 November 2026.
Nominal money of the year stated. No conversions, no deflators, no pakset prices used.

## 1. Method and sources

Searches were run on Exa (`https://api.exa.ai/search`, `type: auto`, highlights), one
subject per search, and every candidate page was then fetched and read before a figure was
recorded. Where a book was identified but not surfaced as readable text, the Internet
Archive catalogue and `_djvu.txt` full text were used instead of a second web search.

Searches run (subjects, in order):

1. Purchase price of a new stage coach 1800-1830 from coachbuilders' bills and coaching histories.
2. Stage coach operating accounts 1820-1840 with repairs separated from horses.
3. Hackney carriage and hansom cab prices and annual repairs, 1830-1860.
4. Horse omnibus purchase price and working expenses, 1830-1880.
5. Post Office mail coach contract rates per mile and price of a mail coach.
6. Select Committee on Hackney Carriages 1831 evidence on cab costs.
7. Archival catalogue entries for coachbuilders' bills with sums stated.
8. Eighteenth-century hackney coach purchase price and yearly upkeep.

Confidence definitions used below:

- **high** — the figure is a sum stated in a priced document, contract, or account and its scope
  is clear from the same passage.
- **medium** — the figure is stated by a contemporary but as an approximation ("about"), or the
  scope needs an assumption.
- **low** — the figure is at second hand (historian, journalist, website), undated, or the
  transcription is damaged.

Evidence type, kept separate from confidence:

- **MEASURED** — a sum actually charged, paid, or contracted.
- **STATED** — a price list, or a writer asserting a price.
- **INFERRED** — my own arithmetic or analogy; never entered in the table as a price.

Notes on individual rows:

- Row 1 and row 3 are the same passage. The coach is priced as a unit; harness is named only in
  the repair figure, which is why harness is treated as separate capital.
- Row 2 is a fleet total. Dividing £210 by three coaches gives £70 per coach per year, which is
  70 per cent of the coach's new price each year. That division is my arithmetic, not a statement
  in the source, and it is not entered as a price.
- Row 4 is the only coaching figure found that prices the coach and its repair together as a
  running charge. Joyce writes "five farthings a mile or 2¾d. a mile out and in". Five farthings
  is 1¼d, and doubling it gives 2½d, so the two halves of the sentence do not agree. Both forms
  are reproduced as stated.
- Rows 5 to 10 are one price list. Felton's "first charge" is explicitly a complete new carriage
  at a defined plain specification: it excludes the boot, the coach box, the raised ends, the
  hammercloth and the harness, each of which he prices separately. It is therefore the closest
  thing found to a body-plus-chassis price with the trimmings stripped out.
- Row 14 is a hire charge, not a repair bill. It is included because it is the only annual figure
  found for a long-distance coach in the 1830s, and because hiring from a coachbuilder was the
  normal arrangement (see section 3).
- Row 15 is a total for the whole mail coach fleet. Dividing by 250 gives about £10 per coach per
  year, but the number of coaches is itself given as approximate ("about 250 per annum"), so the
  division is not entered as a price.
- Row 20 is internally consistent with its own arithmetic (4,500 cabs at £60 is £270,000), which
  supports the reading that £60 covers cab and two horses together. That is a very low figure for
  a four-wheeler in 1860 and it conflicts with rows 21 and 22; it is recorded, not relied on.
- Row 27 is a late revival of coaching as a sport, not commercial mail coaching. It is listed to
  prevent it being mistaken for an 1830s figure.

## 3. What the evidence supports

**The nominal price of a coach was remarkably flat between the 1760s and 1796.** A "good coach"
cost about £100 in the early 1760s and a plain London-built perch coach had a first charge of
£105 9s in 1796. Whatever inflation occurred in the intervening decades is not visible in these
two figures. The 1830 claim of £130-£150 is therefore plausible as a later, better-finished
vehicle, but it is not corroborated by any document found.

**Coaches were frequently hired, not owned.** Harris describes a proprietor paying "expense of
the coaches hired at a rate per mile", and separately describes a proprietor charging "a farthing
or halfpenny a mile more than a coachbuilder would have charged" for the use of his own coaches.
The 1787 Besant arrangement is the same structure: the patentee supplied the coaches and kept them
in thorough repair for a rate per mile, and the contractors paid that rate. For the pakset this
means the purchase price is only one of two historically normal ways of acquiring the vehicle, and
the per-mile hire rate is the better-attested running charge.

**A stage coach had a short life.** Three years, from the 1760s evidence, with the value falling
to about £40 after one year. That is a loss of about £60 in year one and about £40 over the
remaining two years, or roughly £33 a year on average. An omnibus, by contrast, lasted about
twelve years in 1901. The two vehicle types should not share a depreciation assumption.

**Repair cost was large relative to capital for stage coaches.** About £70 per coach per year in
the 1760s against a £100 new price is the only direct figure, and it is consistent with a
three-year life and with Palmer's 1787 complaint that contractors were paying coachmakers so
little that repairs were made with "a clip or a bolt". The same complaint is evidence that repair
spending was being suppressed below the level needed, so the 1760s figure is more likely a
well-maintained upper bound than a typical minimum.

**Omnibus capital was of the same order as coach capital, but the vehicle lasted far longer.**
£100 or a little more in 1853 and £150-£160 in 1901, against a twelve-year life, gives a capital
charge of roughly £10-£13 a year per omnibus. The 1857 LGOC figure of about £700 per omnibus
includes horses, harness and goodwill and is not a vehicle price.

**Harness is a separate and identifiable cost.** Felton prices a coach's wheel harness at about
£11 14s, and Garwood puts an omnibus's harness and appointments at £30. Either way it is a small
addition to the vehicle price, not a large fraction of it.

**Taxes were a substantial annual charge on a stage coach.** £5 a year licence on a four-wheeled
coach in 1776, and from 1783 a mileage duty; by 1838 the mileage duty on stage coaches in England
was £106,625 on 10,530,000 miles, an average of about 2.43d per mile over the whole fleet. That
average is my division of Harper's two stated totals.

## 4. What the evidence does not support

- **No coachbuilder's bill for a new stage coach, 1800-1840, was found.** The £130-£150 figure
  rests on an unattributed web page quoted on a forum. It should not be treated as measured.
- **No annual repair account for a stage coach in the 1820s or 1830s was found.** The only
  period repair figure is the 1760s fleet total, and the only 1830s annual figure is a hire
  charge that bundles capital with repairs.
- **No price for a mail coach as distinct from a stage coach was found.** Mail coaches were built
  to Post Office pattern by a single contractor, so a competitive market price would not be
  expected; the Besant per-mile contract is the nearest documented term.
- **Body-only prices are not available.** Felton prices components separately in his chapter on
  extras, but his "first charge" is a complete carriage. No source found states what a stage coach
  body alone cost.
- **No hackney carriage price before 1830 was found.** Mayhew states that early hackney coaches
  were "nearly all noblemen's and gentlemen's disused family coaches", which suggests the trade
  was supplied from the second-hand private market rather than by new build. That is a statement
  about practice, not a price.
- **No omnibus repair figure was found.** Garwood's £900 a year is a total working expense
  including horses, wages and provender, and cannot be decomposed from the text.
- **No measured figure connects a coach's price to its seating capacity.** Coaches of markedly
  different capacity (a short stage coach and a mail coach) cannot be separated on price evidence.

## 5. Dead ends

1. **Barker & Co account book, 66 Chandos Street, Covent Garden** (City of Westminster Archives
   Centre; TNA Discovery reference c8f1f1ad). Described as 270 pages of priced specifications for
   about seventy orders, including new carriages and repairs. Not digitised; the catalogue entry
   carries no sums. The Science Museum's BARK collection (25 volumes of sales ledgers, 1799-1942)
   is likewise catalogued but not online. These are the most promising unread sources found.
2. **"Sharing account", London and Dorking coach, four weeks to 5 August 1831**, reproduced in
   Harper, *The Portsmouth Road* (1895), p. 119. The only accessible scan is a page-image
   transcription in which every money column is lost; no repair line can be read.
3. **Return of Coaches Carrying Letters in England and Wales**, HC 381, 7 June 1841 (GBPS PDF).
   The return exists and gives the "price paid per double mile to the horsing contractors" for
   each coach for 1837-1841, but the tabular OCR destroys the digits. Legible fragments show rates
   of 1½d to 5d per double mile and annual agreements of £70 to £500 for short branches. Not usable
   as a table without the original paper.
4. **Edenhall (Cumbria) coachmaker's accounts, 1820 and 1823**, transcribed at rumbutter.net. The
   pages render as site navigation only; the transcriptions are not in the HTML text.
5. **Mayhew, *London Labour and the London Poor*, vol. III, "Hackney-Coaches and Cabs"**, via the
   Tufts TEI edition. All money figures are stripped from the transcription, leaving sentences of
   the form "costs from to".
6. **Gerhold, "Stage coaches re-examined" (2019 Clinker Lecture)**, RCHS Journal 237 (2020). Read
   in full for coach costs; it deals with speeds, fares and competition and gives no coach price.
7. **Thrupp, *The History of Coaches* (1877).** Prices only for state coaches (a state coach by
   Runciman and Barker, 1757, £860), which is not a road passenger vehicle price.
8. **Harris, *The Coaching Age*, ch. IX "Construction of Coaches".** Detailed on design and on
   Post Office specification, but no build price.
9. **TNA Discovery and county record office catalogue searches** for coachbuilders' bills. Entries
   describe the documents but do not transcribe the sums.
10. **Hansard, 9 July 1835**, on the return of payments to mail coach contractors: the speaker
    complains that the return gives totals rather than a breakdown, and no per-coach price is
    stated in the debate.

Two structural points for balancing, both supported by Table 1 rather than inferred:

1. A stage coach's economic life in the evidence is three years, an omnibus's about twelve.
   Depreciation per vehicle per year should differ by roughly a factor of four even where the
   purchase prices are similar.
2. Hiring the vehicle at a rate per mile was a normal alternative to owning it, and the 1787
   Besant contract bundles supply with thorough repair at 1¼d per mile. Any pakset running cost
   that is set from repair evidence alone will sit below the historical all-in vehicle charge.

## 7. Currency and scope conventions used

- Sums are reproduced in pounds, shillings and pence as printed, with the original "l." and "£"
  forms both normalised to "£" in the table but left as printed in quotations.
- "Scope: individual" means one vehicle. "Scope: class" means an aggregate over several vehicles
  or a whole fleet, and such figures must not be divided without recording the division as an
  inference.
- No figure in this report has been converted to another year's money, to a price index, or to
  pakset currency.
