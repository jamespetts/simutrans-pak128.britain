# Research pricing report 40 (2026-11-24: vehicle decomposition, five parallel passes)

Five passes supporting [maintenance-decomposition](../vehicle-cost/maintenance-decomposition.md):
(1) overhaul evidence by class, (2) renewals shares and vehicle lives, (3) fixed/distance splits
for rail, (4) vehicle-specific upgrade costs, (5) non-rail fixed/distance splits. Each section is
appended as its pass reports. Notation: **M** measured in source, **S** stated/secondary,
**C** computed here, **L** lead only.

## 1. Overhaul evidence by class

### 1.1 Goods wagons

**New-price denominators**: 1883 GER 5-plank 10/12-ton wagon £112 7s, component split body £70 +
ironwork £21 12s + wheels and axles £20 15s (GERS "Wagons 1880-1889",
https://gersociety.org.uk/rolling-stock/wagons/1880-1889) — the first measured **wheelset share:
18.5% of new** (C). The L&BR 1840 pair (in KB: wheel sets £20/£29 on a £65 wagon) puts early
wheelsets at 30-45%, so use 15-20% after 1880. 1906 Teague PO wagon £66 (KB); 1923 Gloucester Gas
ten 12-ton PO wagons £135 10s each and 1924 twenty at £163 (Pope via
https://glostransporthistory.visit-gloucestershire.co.uk/Pope.htm).

**Annual maintenance money (bridge to per-overhaul)**: Hill 1892 £4 4s/wagon-year repairs+renewals
(KB); Midland **renewal charge** £2 12s/wagon-year in 1896, "£5 a few years before" (*Railway
Magazine* v1 (1897) p.67 via https://steamindex.com/rlymag/rm1on.htm); 1929 Colchester Gas £5 per
wagon-year (Essex D/F 27/2/108, catalogue wording now read: "annual payment of £5 per wagon (£100
in total)"); **1934-39 Wagon Repairs Ltd agreement, Arthur & Edward colliery, about 507 wagons, at
£5 8s 6d per wagon per annum** (M; Lightmoor, *Forest of Dean Coal Mining*, colliery papers COAL36
48, https://lightmoor.co.uk/forestcoal/CoalA&E.html) = 3.3-4% of a contemporary wagon a year;
1920 Midland ROD return-to-traffic repairs averaging £9 per wagon (L — RMweb, verify in source).

**Per-job/component money**: the RCH published schedules exist but are archive-only (Chasewater
4778 "Charges for Private Owner Wagons" 1937; 4733 "Private Wagons Stopped for Repairs" 1926;
HMRS "RCH Wagon repair regulations, 1916"). Substitutes: wheel/axle sets 18.5% of new (1883);
tyre method anchors from GER locomotive practice 1888-90 (new tyres £10/ton with scrap credit;
54 lb tyre per 1,000 engine-miles; re-turned when flanges wore — Hill 1895, ICE Min. Proc. 122
pp.232-235); re-timbering unpriced, bounded by the 81.5% body+ironwork share. Structural analogue:
Indian Railways *Maintenance Manual for Wagons* (IRCAMTECH 2022) — POH 4-6 years first / 3.5-6
subsequent, ROH 18-24 months (lift body, bogies out, wheel turning at ROH, axle ultrasonic at
every POH), repairs graded ≤8 / 8-100 / >100 man-hours.

**Intervals**: repair contracts ran in **7-year terms** (documented 1889-1936);
GRCW/Wagon Repairs practice; BR repair-plate regime with permitted intervals and money limits (L);
PO wagons examined before each journey (Findlay 1889, ch.9) — routine exam, not overhaul.

**Composed proposal** (basis recorded): heavy overhaul interval **5-7 years**, cost share
**10-15% of new initial, 20% max**. Check (C): the measured £5-£5 8s 6d annual contract (3.2-4% of
new a year) over a 5-6-year cycle is 16-24% of new covering everything, so a heavy overhaul at
10-15% plus running repairs reconciles. What would move it: the RCH per-job schedules or Wagon
Repairs contracts (archive reading room) would sharpen the share within 8-25%.

### 1.2 Carriages and locomotives

- **BR Mk 1 works overhaul 1982: "more than £2,000"** per vehicle, York Works (SVR 25346 records,
  https://svrwiki.com/BR_25346_Standard_Corridor) ≈ **8% of equivalent new** (C, method-sensitive)
  — corroborates the 8% first / 27% late carriage band.
- **GWR 28xx verified in source** (2874 Trust / TNA engine record card): build £4,992 (£6,193 with
  tender, 1918); Standard No. 1 boiler new £971; 1930s heavy general **£450-700** (9-14% of new);
  boiler overhaul £150-700. Works history: Intermediate Repair about 2 years from traffic, Heavy
  Overhaul year 5, Heavy General year 7, boiler changed at nearly every works visit.
- **Heavy vs light repair scope defined** (Fowler 1913, J. Instn Loco. Engrs 3, pp.1-15 via
  https://steamindex.com/jile/trans.htm): heavy = re-boilered, or boiler out for general repair, or
  new tyres on 4+ wheels, or any two of {new cylinders; new axles; re-tubing; wheels turned plus
  boxes refitted or motion/brake overhauled; in-frame boiler repair with 15%+ stays renewed};
  light = any single item. This is the field mapping for "intermediate vs heavy general".
- **Ministerial intervals, citable**: Hansard 11 Mar 1996 (Mr Watts): coaching-stock full overhaul
  mileage-based "approximately every two years"; locomotives at "two and three-quarter, five and a
  half and 11-year intervals"; carriage exams at 3-4 day/1/3/6/12-month intervals.
- New-build bonus: 1960-61 Met-Cammell Pullman cars £17,000 each (SREMG coupe SE13).

### 1.4 Road vehicles

Lorry engine-exchange money (CM archive, M): Cummins "ReCon" exchange units net with trade-in —
V6 140 £394, V8 185 £473; components fuel pump £31 15s, injector £8 10s, compressor £26 9s, water
pump £8 12s, heads £39 15s/£43 15s; 20% surcharge on rejected blocks (CM 25 Apr 1969 p.44).

Gardner "Power Exchange" exists without prices (CM 10 Nov 1988 p.8). Intervals (M, CM): a fleet
with complete overhauls (engine + body) every **250,000 miles**, repaint every 2 years (CM 1 Apr

and two petrol engine replacements per vehicle life (CM 9 Jan 1959 p.76). Bus £2,000/4-year
Aldenham overhaul already verified (reports 31/37).

### 1.5 Ships and aircraft

**Ships** (M): five-year class special surveys. ICF Kaiser (1995) via US National Academies,
*Double-Hull Tanker Legislation* App. J: minimum **$435,000 drydock + survey** for an excellently
maintained vessel, "typically several million dollars more" with steel renewal; against $33.5-85m
new double-hull tankers = 0.5-1.3% bare, about 3-9% with repairs. Corroboration: Gratsos &
Zachariadis (Hellenic Chamber of Shipping) provision $450,000 per scheduled repair period on a
$31m Panamax newbuild = 1.5% excluding steel renewal. So the KB's 2-5% of new is the with-repairs
typical; bare survey+dock is under 1.5%.

**Aircraft** (M, *Aircraft Commerce* 777 guide issue 60): C1-C6 base cycle — C1/C2/C4/C5
2,400-2,800 MH + $150-175k materials; C3 5,500-6,000 MH + $250k; heavy **C6 17,000-18,000 MH +
$700-800k materials ≈ $1.55-1.7m at $50/MH**; cycle total $3.4-3.6m including strip/repaint
($160-180k), amortised over 23,000-35,000 flight hours. Against a new 777 ($150-220m) the heavy
check is 0.8-1.1% of new and the cycle 1.6-2.4% — **excluding engines** (shop visit $4.2-5.2m a
shipset) and landing gear (10-12% of maintenance cost, half brakes); with heavy components the
event reaches the KB's 2-5% band. D-check "once every six to ten years, over 50,000 man-hours",
findings add 10-20% (AviTrader MRO360 Nov 2025, Vallair).

  against £126 new in 1861** (≈91%) — "may represent in effect the cost of overhaul"; note for the

  also 63 cars repaired for £2,427 in 1887 (£38.52 a car) and horse "renewal" once every 7 years
  at purchase price (Leeds 1894-5).
- **Tram overhauls**: E/1 class **£400 a tram** (250 for £100,000, 1934, post #119); Feltham
  overhaul "in the region of £300" against a £500 secondhand price (1949, post #121).
- **Ships**: "Earl of Essex" overhauled 1877 with new boilers and other repairs **£1,100 against
  £4,154 new** (≈26%, post #214; Peter Kay, LTSR vol 6 p.486); "Sir Walter Raleigh" repairs
  "effectively an overhaul" £2,840 (1879); tugs new boilers + major engine-room overhaul £4,000

- **Maintenance against age** (post #212): cost multiplier 0.4 new → 1.0 at 6 years → 1.2 at 12 →

the Hornsey post is **#267** (citing an unrecorded book); "Class 42 (Deltic)" conflated two

Calder (Brush), internal dates 1976-77 (recovered via Wayback; the live TRB host refuses TLS).

specifications and frequencies apply to other engines and coaches"): daily visual check;
locomotive detailed exam **every 120 hours in traffic (10-14 days)**; progressively larger exams on
hours; locomotive overhauls at **≈2.75, 5.5 and 11 years**; coaching stock exams every 3-4 days and
at 1/3/6/12 months, full overhaul ≈ every 2 years. TRB SR180: electric exams **14/42/84/168 days +
annual** (time-based); diesel-electric exams on engine hours — **Class 47 at 55/275/825/2,500/5,000

**C4/C6 structure (MUs)**: C1-C6 originate as 1966 workshop coded repairs (railcar.co.uk

includes bogie/engine changes and **C4**, body work, painting and **C6**. Measured C4 mileages:

84-day detailed, **main works every 2 years**; BR 87264/1 (1965) DMU exams "from 625 miles up to
five yearly".

**Per-class highlights**: Class 47 — EWS-era "equivalent major work every seven years, costing up
to £400,000" (Railway Gazette 1998); Class 66 — **1.6m km between major rebuilds** (≈18 years),
rebuild £200,000; Class 55 — five-year fixed-price English Electric service contract 1961-66 with
engines exchanged (D9000 ran 827,055 miles in it); Class 50 — 1979-83 Doncaster refurbishment of
all 50 ("renewal and revision rather than just maintenance"), lease assumed 2/50 permanently in
works; HST power cars (Class 43) — 1976-78 annual Derby works (15 working days), 2016 ladder
A/B/C/D at 7,000/21,000/84,000/252,000 miles with bogies at 630,000 and engine remanufacture at
25,000 h (IMechE 1978; Rail Engineer 2016); Class 390 Pendolino — B/C every 20,000 miles, D
100,000, E/F/G 200,000, heavies H1 750,000 / H2 +850,000 / H3 +950,000 miles (H3 = 2 weeks + C4
bogie overhaul); Class 158 — BR procurement spec "interval between major overhauls of five years
or 350,000 miles" and 13,500 miles between major services (design figures, the source of the
forum's video claims); Class 800/801/802 — daily inspections, 10-day interval exam, 50-day intensive
exam, **heavy overhauls envisaged at ~950 days**, moving to condition-based (Modern Railways 2018;
Rail Engineer 2017/2019); Eurostar 373 — GVG heavy maintenance every 36 months, 11-15 days (L&CI
Temple Mills).

**Engine mapping (per class)**: the engine holds one interval plus the sigmoid. Locomotives:
interval = the **first works event (2.75 yr) converted at the class's k** (`interval_km = 2.75 x
k`; k from TRB or class duty), `overhauls_before_max_cost` = 4 to reach the 11-year event; class
overrides (47: heavy at 7 years → rungs 2-3; 66: 1.6m km, rungs high or interval long; HST: use
the D event 252,000 miles as the interval). MUs second-gen: interval = C4 (~4 years; 325-450k
miles measured), rungs 1-2 to C6; first-gen: 2-year works (EMU) or the 5-yearly top exam (DMU);
third-gen IET: the 950-day heavy as the interval event. Electric locos: 2.75-yr event at class k
(87 at 274,000 km/yr).

### 1.8 Per-class overhaul intervals: steam locomotives

**Company practice (measured)**: GWR engine-history codes G/H/I/L (BR adds HG/HI/LI/C/U); Pendennis
Castle at Swindon about **once a year** over 40 years across all grades (record sheets, Didcot);
"major shopping every 2-3 years" for large locos (gwr.org.uk). GWR 2874 record card, corrected
reading: Intermediate yr 2 (22 months), Heavy Overhaul yr 6.5, **Heavy General yr 13-14**, then IR
1939/1947 and Heavy Intermediate 1957; boiler exchange every 38,349-109,463 miles (mean 83,901).
**LMS 1930s (the citable replacement for the forum's 5XP video)** — Stanier in discussion, JILocoE
43 (1953) p.218: "engines were called in for intermediate repair at about 60,000 to 70,000 miles,
and given general repair at about 110,000 to 130,000 miles". LMS terms: casual/service/general
repair, mileage weighed against the defect list (Ball, JILocoE 29 (1938)). LNER record cards:
Light Repair / General Overhaul / Heavy Repair; A4s took G.O. about 2 years from new, 44-55 days in
works (SNG Trust); **monthly** shed examinations (Gresley). SR: Leader's brief "not less than
100,000 miles between general overhauls" (Bulleid, Proc IMechE 156 (1947)); Urie 4-6-0s 225,000
miles between generals (Bond's caveat: shop work between generals may sit outside the count).
BR 1948-53: classified repairs called in **by condition** ("wide variations… preclude the use of
average mileage between repairs as a satisfactory basis" — Bond); all-engine averages: general to
general **101,000 mi**, general to intermediate **64,000 mi**; WR general interval 80,000 mi /
41 months; intervals up nearly 30% over 30 years. **BR Standard months between shop repairs
(Bond)**: Cl7 15 · Cl6 18 · Cl5 24 · Cl4 4-6-0 28 · Cl4 2-6-0 30 · Cl2 2-6-0 36 · Cl4 2-6-4T 28 ·
Cl3 2-6-2T 33 · Cl2 2-6-2T 36. Crewe works time averaged 24 days.

to 53 days (MN 1950s); mid-value 1.5-2 months for `overhaul_month_tenths`; Crewe average 24 days.

**Ladder mapping**: Hart-Davies' model (intermediate at the halfway mark of the general cycle)
supports `overhauls_before_max_cost` = 2 with the interval field = the intermediate distance (Bond
Table IV per class), the sigmoid reaching max at the heavy general / boiler-exchange event.

**Leads and caveats**: no GWR "Special" grade exists (codes G/H/I/L); the 5XP "12 days in works"
stays uncited; Bond's companion paper "Locomotive Maintenance" (c.1952) is unidentified — likely
the classified-repair definitions; industrial intervals rest on rung 3/5; Pendennis Castle
mileages are the sheets' own estimates ±10-15%; the 1925 "Classification of repairs" is a facsimile
form. RMweb, Modern Railways, HathiTrust, RCH per-job schedules: settled dead ends, not retried.

### 1.9 Carriage overhaul by era; wagon and bus era notes

**Retrieval fix (closes an open item):** the railcar.co.uk PDFs are not dead — a browser-header
fetch with `Referer` retrieves them. Both are now read: *Workshops Overhaul Procedure — Carriages
Coded Repairs* (June 1966, the C1-C6 definitions) and *Documentation for the Shopping…* (Apr

**Victorian 4/6-wheel wooden (1830s-90s)**: NER works throughput 1892 (*Round the Works of our
Great Railways* 1893, pp.102-104): 200 new carriages, **100 rebuilt**, **8,700 repaired** on stock

renewed, 75,000 repaired on 79,366 ⇒ 0.95 visits a wagon-year — near-annual light attention; the

second treatment about three more, then painted (Stratford coach painters' chapter); GER average
carriage age 9.6 years (1891). L&YR 1850: replacement fund arithmetic gives a **20-year renewal
duration** (Hawkshaw report). Larkin & Larkin: lifetime main-works repairs ≈ 4× original cost
(publisher abstract; chapter paywalled). The Lacy & Dow £62 event (≈25% of capital) maps onto C1
minus the lift. Envelope: Hill's works spend (≈4.5-5.5% of new a year including ≈2.5% renewals)
forces a 25% event to ≥5-year spacing.

**Grouping steel-panelled (1920s-40s)**: GWR 1935 capacity (*Swindon Works and its place in GWR
History*, Grace's Guide): 8,948 coaching vehicles, works capacity 275 new + 5,000 repairs a year
(3,000 heavy) ⇒ works visit every ~1.8 yr, **heavy repair every ~3.0 yr** (computed). LMS St Rollox
June 1945: heavy carriage output 36 a week (≈1,870/yr), bogie stock 1 vehicle/hour progressive
lifting (war-backlog caveat). Progressive repair cut carriage repair costs 30% (LMS 1923-46
record). LMS 1945: 22,379 passenger train vehicles, replacement £49.5m (≈£2,212 average).

**BR Mk 1/2 (1951-75) — the two-speed cycle (all MEASURED, 1965 code + 1966 coded repairs)**:
Code of Practice M.C.65/80 (1 Dec 1965): Class A/B/C stock; **full renovation (complete exterior
repaint + full interior renovation) between years 9-11 of life; second complete overhaul years
19-21**; one spray coat on ≤3 occasions between; target throughput one-tenth of Class A a year;
post-second-overhaul work over £500 (age 21-25) / £250 (26+) needs central authority. **Coded
repair scopes**: C1 = lift, recondition bogies/brakes/underframe, body repairs, full re-trim,
complete repaint; C2 = same with part re-trim; C3 = lift + running gear + necessary body/trim/
paint; C4 = lighter; C5 = minor bogie/underframe incl. wheel turning; C6 = body repairs, not
lifted. **Target days on works: C1 20, C2 18, C3 14, C4 10** (1966 averages 18-76 by works).
Repairs applied "on condition" at the lowest applicable code. **Reconciliation**: Hansard 1996's
"full overhaul approximately every two years" is the coded-visit cadence; the 1965 decennial full
renovation is the top event — one ladder, not a conflict. Distance: 2-yr visits ≈ 80,000-160,000
km at 25-50,000 mi/yr coaching duty (correcting the earlier 250,000 km draft).

**BR Mk 3+ (1975-2000s)**: original regime annual workshop overhaul at Derby, 15 working days,
target 3 weeks out of service (IMechE 1978, Power, "High Speed Train — Fleet Operation"); later
regime (Rail Engineer 2016): A/B exams 15,000/30,000 mi, balanced exam 90,000, **C4 bogie at
600,000→750,000 mi** in a day at depot; tyres turned 250,000/500,000 mi, new wheels at 750,000.
Heavy body/structural event ≈10-12 years (Chiltern rebuild, corrosion repair "at least 10 to 12
years"). HMRS holds "Mk III, HST & Mk IIF coaches, draft overhaul specification" (1993) — the one
document giving Mk 3 coded intervals and scopes; archive enquiry.

**Modern (2000s-2026)**: IET (Hitachi AT300): daily inspections, 10-day interval exam, 50-day
intensive exam, **heavier overhauls "envisaged at around the 950-day mark"** (Modern Railways 27

### 1.10 Comet 1 pressurisation-cycle fatigue

**Citation correction**: the Court of Inquiry report is **C.A.P. 127** (MT & CA, 1955, Lord
Cohen), not Cmd 9018; the RAE evidence is *Report on Comet Accident Investigation* (Accident Note
270, 1954). All read in full (2026-11-24).

**Fatigue cycles** (= one pressurisation = one pressurised flight): G-ALYP failed on her
**1,286th** pressurised flight (10 Jan 1954, plus 255 unpressurised; fatigue crack from a 10 mm
bolthole at the rear ADF window cut-out); G-ALYY on her **903rd** (8 Apr 1954). FAA's summary:
production Comets formed fatigue cracks near window corners at **approximately 1,000 flight
cycles**. The RAE tank airframe G-ALYU (1,221-1,230 pressurisations and about 3,530 hours in
service) failed after a further 1,830 tank cycles — **3,057-3,060 total, about 2,500
service-equivalent** per RAE's Walker (the tank omits vibration and ground-air-ground inputs);
testing continued to 5,546 cycles. Nominally identical structures show at least a 9:1 fatigue-life
scatter (C.A.P. 127 paras 70-71). The misleading number: de Havilland's forward-cabin test
section survived 18,000 applications of working pressure — but it had first been taken to 2P,
cold-working the high-stress corners. The design assumption was **10,000 pressurised flights or
10 years** (para 121): the failure was about ten times worse than designed.

**Duty**: BOAC London-Johannesburg (six sectors via Rome, Beirut, Khartoum, Entebbe, Livingstone;
official air miles): **mean sector ≈ 1,790 km** (978-2,229); G-ALYP flew 1,286 pressurised cycles
in 618 days ≈ **758 cycles a year**, ≈2.86 h a cycle (C.A.P. 127 para 23 corroborates: "no Comet
had exceeded 2,500 hours flying — say 800 pressurised flights"). Air France Comet 1As on mixed
sectors (Algiers 1,339 km etc.): F-BGNZ flew 371 h / 211 landings in four months. C.A.P. 127's own
equivalence: 1 flight ≈ 3 hours.

**Comet 4 exclusion**: the post-accident design (thicker cabin skin, redesigned oval windows,
complete-cabin fatigue testing) targeted a safe life of at least 30,000 hours; Comet 4s flew
commercially 1958-1980 and the cycle-heavy Comet 4B needed only minor structural changes. The
100% rule must not touch them. Regulatory outcome (for the record): C.A.P. 127 Part XV required
complete-cabin tank testing; BCAR D3-7 (1956) reg 4.3.2 required the test cabin never to have been
used for strength work — the cold-working lesson. No "1,000-cycle rule" exists in the inquiry.

**Pakset rule**: the Comet 1 (`dh-comet-1`) overhaul is **replacement of the fatigue-doomed
airframe at ≈1,000 pressurisation cycles** (band 900-1,300), entered as 1,790,000 km = 1,000
cycles × the measured BOAC mean sector 1,790 km — **sector-dependent**: shorter sectors reach the
cycle limit sooner per km (the engine triggers on distance only). Cost revised same day at the
user's direction: **67% of new, not 100%**, because the Ghost engines, avionics and interior
fittings are salvageable (gross salvage 40-45% of new = engines 25-30% + avionics 8-10% +
interior 7-10%, less 8-12% extraction and refit = net 30-35%); band 65-75, rung 3/5. Direction:
a Ghost engine unit price or de Havilland price list would sharpen the salvage share.

**The money-life correction.** Price Williams's "money life" (10.81 years etc.) is **not** a

crank axles 6 yr, tyres 7 yr, boiler/axle-boxes/firebox 10 yr, framing group 30 yr ≈ £24.6/yr
amortised) — what the 1911 form calls "repairs and partial renewals". The capital share to net out
is **net cost ÷ service life ≈ £60-75/engine-year ≈ 25% of £253**, not the full £139.91.

## 3. Fixed/distance splits, rail

**No measured fixed/distance split exists for any British rolling stock** — but three independent
indicators converge on fixed-majority for wagons and carriages and near-even for modern traction.

**Hill's Appendix II Tables IV (GER) and V (LCDR)** (ICE Min. Proc. 122 pp. 244-245; IA
`minutesproceedi43britgoog` leaves 258-259; IIIF page images, OCR layer unusable for tables) give
half-yearly maintenance Dec 1887-June 1892 with **wages and materials separate and
passenger/goods train-miles printed in the table** — Table V was not previously recorded in the KB.
The within-company regression fails to identify the split (goods miles per wagon-year is invariant
418-455, mean 438, ±4%, so stock and miles are collinear), but the table's behaviour is itself the
finding: **wages track stock almost exactly (+9.6% wages vs +9.5% stock, 1887→92) and are 45% of
wages+materials; materials (55%) swing seasonally; December halves cost 2.91d against 2.10d per
goods train-mile (+39%) on about 5% more miles.** Cost variation is not mileage-driven. Hill's own
text agrees: "There does not appear to be much relation between the cost of maintenance and the
number of train-miles per engine" (p. 227).

**Renewals shares** (Hill Appendix III Tables XXIII-XXV, renewal rates by number, 5 years to June
1892): engines **3.6%/yr** (≈28-year life), carriages **2.53%/yr**, wagons **3.1%/yr** (range
1.2-6.4% by company; GER 2.0%, Midland 6.4%). Against new-build prices this makes **30-40% of
Hill's "maintenance" totals renewals** — capital in-game, to be netted out before any split. The
measured route to the exact share remains the post-1911 returns' "Complete renewals" against
"Repairs and partial renewals" columns; under the 1928 Accounts Order both Abstract B (Maintenance
of Rolling Stock) and Abstract C (Locomotive Running Expenses) carry "Transfer to Renewal Account".

**Distance-wearing items, measured shares**: steam locomotive tyres 54 lb per 1,000 engine-miles ≈
0.052d/mile (GER 1888-90, net of scrap) with tyre lives 168,012 mi express / 105,444 suburban /
216,090 goods; Midland boiler renewal £400 per 400,000 miles ≈ 0.24d/mile; crank axles special
exam at 250,000 mi; painting six-monthly. Tyres + boiler ≈ 12% of the ~2.5d per train-mile total
are known distance items. Wagons: wheelset overhaul every **250,000 km** (alternating reprofiling),
heavy at 1,000,000 km ≈ €1,800/axle (UIP WG 2011 §3.5); P6 wheel turning 25,000 miles (RSSB);
passenger stock wheels turned at 140,000 miles or annually.

**The measured contractual form** (modern): wagon lease and full-service maintenance = **standing
fee per wagon-period + an assumed 50,000 km/wagon-year with excess-mileage charges** (Railrelease
GTC cl. 5.3-5.4; Railtrans GTC cl. 10 at EUR 0.04/wagon-km over). The LL brake-block retrofit is
published in both forms of the same increment: 300-600 EUR/wagon-year **or** 0.01-0.02 EUR/wagon-km.
ORR market study (2009): wagon lease 50-60% of annual wagon costs, maintenance 40-50%. GB
passenger leases: Porterbrook 2024 accounts — rentals are fixed "other than for indexation and
mileage"; the 2009 Transparency Order Schedule 6 requires reserve charges to state use assumptions
and the adjustment basis. HS2 maintenance priced per unit-km, with the classic-compatibility
premium explicitly attributed to wheel turning and wheelset replacement.

**ORR Rolling Stock Maintenance Market Review, interim (29 Oct 2026)**: 87 fleets, 17 TOCs,
2024-25 — median £/vehicle-mile TSA first term 0.72, TSA subsequent 1.88, TSSSA 1.42, in-house
1.73 (£/vehicle-year £93k-285k). ORR states fixed costs make smaller or less-utilised fleets look
dear per mile, and finds higher fleet mileage → lower cost per vehicle-mile, highly significant in
all OLS specifications. The coefficients are not yet published: the final report is the single
document that would convert directly into F and V.

**Dead ends (do not repeat)**: the econometric cost-function literature is entirely
infrastructure (Wheat & Smith 2008, CATRIN 2009, CEPA/ORR 2025 — no rolling-stock function exists
publicly); ORR/SDG 2015 publishes no split; Transparency Order reviews contain no rates; ROSCO
accounts disclose the charge form and balances but no per-vehicle rates; Hill IV/V via OCR text is
unusable — read the IIIF page images. **On-target and unmined**: BR Research reports IM-TAG-005

### 4.4 Mapping to the engine's upgrade_price

Definitions: C = target vehicle's cost (the engine's default `upgrade_price`); B = measured works
bill; R = the old vehicle's resale credit the engine withholds; V' = market value of the vehicle the
real rebuild produces; V_old = value of the old vehicle consumed. Equating wealth changes:
**U = B + (C - V') + (V_old - R)**. Practical cases:

1. **Donor-inclusive totals** (Class 47->57 GBP 500k including the donor): the donor purchase IS
   V_old, so **U = measured total**.
2. **In-place works on an owned vehicle** (brake fitting, reboilering, Greenway, sliding doors,
   re-tractioning, repower): the rebuild consumes the vehicle into the rebuilt asset; with V' about
   equal to C, **U = B**. E.g. fitted brakes: U = GBP 35 a carriage = 7.7% of the unfitted price.
3. **Partial-renewal events** (Supertram life extension, Metrolink mid-life): V' < C, so U sits
   above B by (C - V'); where V' is unmeasurable enter **max(B, class rule x C)** and note the game
   grants a full fresh life.
4. **Cap**: U <= C; never above buying new.

Reducing U below B by R (because the game deletes the old vehicle) is **rejected** — it
double-counts, since the real rebuild also consumes the old vehicle into the rebuilt asset. The one
genuine semantic gap runs upward: if the engine reissues the target at age zero while real rebuilds
confer partial renewal, U rises by (C - V'). **Open engine question: does an upgrade reset vehicle
age?** `[UNVERIFIED]`

## 5. Fixed/distance splits, non-rail

**Headline: the 1959 *Commercial Motor* bus comparison is recovered** — report 31's "dead end for
values" closed by the report-39 §6 scan method. CM 2 January 1959, pp. 62, 65 ("Comparison of Bus
Operating Costs"; https://archive.commercialmotor.com/article/2nd-january-1959/62/hidden-subsidies-in-rural-bus-services)
gives standing costs per week and running costs per mile at **200, 400 and 600 miles a week** for
a 14-seat petrol, 31-seat petrol and 44-seat oil bus, and its prose states the structure: "this
item consists of two elements. One is the repair work, which varies directly with the mileage run,
and the other is servicing and washing, which are normally scheduled on a time basis." It is the
only measured fixed/distance decomposition of road-vehicle maintenance found anywhere.

**Decomposition of the maintenance item** (per-mile maintenance = variable + standing/week ÷
miles/week; pairwise fits on the three printed points; all rows sum to the printed totals):

| Vehicle | Standing maintenance | Variable | Fixed share at 200/400/600 mi a week (10.4k/20.8k/31.2k mi/yr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14-seat petrol | 220-312d/week (LSQ 238d ≈ 19s 10d; ≈ £52/yr) | 1.2-1.45 d/mi | 47% / 30% / 23% |
| 31-seat petrol | 380-384d/week (≈ £83/yr) | 2.19-2.20 d/mi | 47% / 30% / 22% |
| 44-seat oil | 396-404d/week (≈ £87/yr) | 2.05-2.07 d/mi | 49% / 33% / 24% |

**Lorries** — FTA *Managers' Guide to Distribution Costs* 2016, three mileage bands recovered via
CLOCS/WSP (https://clocs.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/ICIU-HGVs-Final-Report.pdf pp. 9-10):
32-t rigid tipper maintenance £11,358/yr @50,000 mi, £11,751 @55,000, £13,345 @65,000 → standing
≈ £4,400 a vehicle-year (LSQ £4,428) + 13.6p/mile (LSQ); fixed 33-39% at those duties (fit
imperfect: the bands differ in tyre and depreciation life). Artic: tractor £7,290/yr at 70,000 mi
and trailer £4,002/yr, single band, not decomposable.

**Aircraft** — measured leasing-reserve structure (Aircraft Monitor 2012, Appendix C, verified;
IATA *Guidance Material for Aircraft Leases* 4.1 §2.5.4, verified): airframe checks **per calendar
month** ($11,250 + $6,250/month, A320), landing gear **mixed** ($20/FC but not less than
$3,500/month), engine performance restoration **per flight hour** ($88-165/FH/engine), engine LLPs
per flight cycle, APU $38 per running hour. Computed A320 budget at 2,800 FH/yr and 1,850 FC/yr:
time-driven $965k (line/ramp checks $595k + base-check cycle $370k) against usage-driven $1.0-1.3m
(heavy components $180/FC, rotables $170-180/FH, engines about 40% of the bill) → **fixed 30-35%
at normal utilisation**, rising toward 50%+ at low utilisation. Structural corroboration: IATA MCX
FY2024 p. 20 (Oliver Wyman): engine 50% and components 18% of MRO spend are utilisation-driven;
airframe 17% is "more influenced by calendar intervals, aircraft age, and fleet management".

**Ships** — calendar pieces measured: drydocking US$1.2-2.3m per 2.5-5-year cycle (2020 Bulkers
2025 filings; Moore Stephens OpCost); Drewry's own annualisation ("intermediate survey and dry
docking divided by five"); Kirkaldy 1913 annual overhaul £30,000 on £1.5m capital = 2%/yr,
explicitly calendar. Running repairs + spares only measured for 2003 (OpCost handysize $162,196/yr,
head includes docking allowances). The Drewry R&M/drydocking split (App. 11/12) stays paywalled.
**Recommended 50-65% standing for motor ships.**

**Trams** — no measured split; recommended **40-55% standing**: modern contracts are per
vehicle-year availability payments (NET: Alstom €120m for 37 trams over 23 years, corroborating
the £118,657/tram-year), overhauls are calendar-based, and buses at similar duty measure 22-33%.

**Standing-only classes** (user direction 2026-11-24, levels confirmed): horses — keep £28-60/yr,
central £45 (London accounts 1876-97, excluding the horsekeeper's wages); horse vehicles — all
repair accounts per-year (1760s coach £70 a coach-year, about 70% of capital; no horse-drawn goods
repair account exists — report 31 dead end 4 stands); sailing craft — Martin's 5%-of-capital/yr
working figure, the one measured component being sheathing £120/yr on a 400-500-ton ship (1862,
Mercer, HSLC); measured levels would come from the barque account books at Royal Museums Greenwich
(*ELEANOR DODSON* AMS/36; *BURNS* MSS/77/063).

**The rule the measured data imposes**: do not set a fixed *percentage*; set an absolute standing
sum per vehicle-year plus a per-km rate. The fixed share is a function of duty (47% → 22% as bus
mileage goes 200 → 600 a week), so a percentage is only correct at one duty. The existing 75/25
distance/standing rule matches the measured shares at busy urban duty and understates standing for
low-duty and rural vehicles (measured 35-50% at 15-25,000 km/yr).

**Tried and failed / confirmed dead ends**: Drewry SOC structure confirmed but values paywalled

withheld under s.43(2) (same as FOI-2321-1718, FOI-3107-2526); IATA MCX segment tables are
members-only (the public p. 20 carries the Oliver Wyman shares used above); Metrolink O&M 2027
tender £1.6bn is operation+maintenance combined, not decomposable; US transit literature classifies
maintenance wholly as per-mile and states no fixed element (the CM 1959 decomposition remains
