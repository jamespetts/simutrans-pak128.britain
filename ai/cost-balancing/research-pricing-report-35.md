# Modern UK light rail and tram rolling stock: purchase prices and maintenance, 1990-2026

Bounded research pass, 23 November 2026. Companion file: `ai/temp/tram-rows-modern.csv`.

## 1. Method

- Searches were run on Exa (`https://api.exa.ai/search`, `type: auto`, `contents.highlights`), one query per research step, with the subject, constraint, date window and source preference in the query text.
- Every figure below was read on the page cited. No figure is taken from a search-result highlight alone unless the highlight is itself the full sentence from the page (contract notices and press releases often are).
- Confidence tags follow the project convention used in this pass:
  - **MEASURED** — a transaction price reported for a contract, a final account, or a procurement award. In practice only two of the figures below come from primary documents (the Contracts Finder award notice and the Edinburgh final account); the rest are transaction prices reported by a party to the contract or by trade press reporting the award, which is what "measured" is intended to distinguish from a later assertion or an estimate.
  - **STATED** — a figure asserted by an officer, manufacturer, journalist or historian without any contract behind it being visible.
  - **COMPUTED** — my division of two stated figures; the arithmetic is shown.
  - **ESTIMATE** — someone's forward or indicative estimate, not a transaction.
- No conversion of any kind has been performed. All money is nominal, in the currency and year of the source (GBP unless the source states EUR or USD). No pakset `.dat` cost, runningcost or fixed_cost value was read, and no pakset price is used in any calculation. The pakset ladder quoted in section 5 is the ladder supplied in the task brief, not a value read from the repository.
- Denominators (vehicle-km) that would normally come from the DfT `LRT0105`/`LRT0106` tables were not loaded, so the only per-vehicle-km figure below rests on a per-tram annual mileage stated in a secondary source. That is flagged where it appears.

What the maintenance evidence supports.

- The Nottingham figure is the strongest item: GBP 118,657 per tram-year, in 2011 money, for maintenance of a 37-tram fleet by the manufacturer, over 23 years. The Alstom euro figure (€120m) independently produces €141k per tram-year, so the two statements are consistent at the exchange rate of the day. It is a **contract price**, not an outturn cost, and it is an average over a 23-year term that includes heavy overhaul years.
- The implied GBP 1.32 per tram-km is the only per-vehicle-km figure in this pass and it depends on an assumed 90,000 km per tram-year. Treat it as an order of magnitude (roughly GBP 1-1.5 per tram-km) until a measured annual mileage is substituted.
- Sheffield confirms the shape of the pakset's existing Sheffield figure: maintenance is about 12% of a GBP 23.6m operating budget, and that 12% covers trams, track and infrastructure together. Per tram-year it is about GBP 88,500 on a combined basis, i.e. the rolling-stock share must be materially below GBP 88,500.
- The Tyne and Wear residual is an upper bound. If the true rolling-stock maintenance element were half of it, the figure would be about GBP 105,000 per train-year, which sits close to the Nottingham figure after allowing for a five-car metro train instead of a 33 m tram.
- No UK operator publishes a rolling-stock maintenance line in its published accounts that I could reach. TfGM, Nexus, SYMCA, WMCA, Tramlink Nottingham and Edinburgh Trams all report at whole-system or whole-company level; the CoMET/Nova benchmarking publishes rolling-stock maintenance per car-km only as charts.

## 4. Are the historic and modern figures separable, and on what basis?

They are separable as **transactions** but not as **like-for-like machines**. Three normalisations were tested.

**Per vehicle.** This is the only basis on which both ends of the period have hard numbers, and it is the basis the pakset uses. It fails to compare like with like: a 1902 municipal car is a four-wheel open-top double-decker seating 45-56; a 2015 tram is a 30-43 m articulated unit with 200-250 places. Per vehicle, the ratio between the two ends is roughly 1:3,500 in nominal money (GBP 590 in 1901 against GBP 2,062,500 in 2009), which is mostly a price-level effect and cannot be interpreted as a real cost increase.

**Per seat.** Two points can be computed because both price and seating are stated for the same vehicle:
- Blackpool 249 (1934): GBP 2,688, 94 seats → GBP 28.60 per seat.
- Edinburgh Urbos 3 (2014): GBP 2,396,074, 78 seats (250 total places) → GBP 30,719 per seat, or GBP 9,584 per total-capacity place.

These are the only pair in the set for which both price and seating are known, so the comparison is 1934 against 2014. On that pair the ratio is 891:1 per vehicle and 1,074:1 per seat: seat normalisation **widens** the gap by about 20 per cent, because the modern tram has fewer seats than the 1930s double-decker. Per total-capacity place (standing included) the modern tram is cheaper per place than per seat, because most of its capacity is standing. So capacity normalisation does not close the gap and does not explain the era break.

**Per metre of vehicle.** Not usable. Lengths are available for the modern vehicles (Croydon CR4000 30.1 m; Nottingham Citadis 32.6 m; Sheffield 34.75 m; Edinburgh 42.8 m) but not consistently for the historic cars in the sources read, and length is a poor proxy for cost because the cost drivers are the truck, the motors, the controller and the body finish, not the metres of framing.

**Conclusion.** The defensible normalisation is per vehicle, with the vehicle class stated alongside (seats, length, articulation), and with the price expressed in the money of its own year. Price per seat is a useful secondary check but cannot carry the ladder on its own, because the seat count of a modern tram is lower than that of a 1930s double-decker while its total capacity is much higher.

## 5. The era break: what the measured series looks like, and where the pakset ladder sits

Measured or stated prices per vehicle, nominal:

| 1900s (period estimate) | GBP 700 | 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Tramway" (E. Garcke): capital cost table uses "Cars at £700 each" |
| 1926-1934 | GBP 1,750-2,688 | Blackpool Standard, Hurst Nelson, GBP 1,750 (1926); Blackpool Pullman, GBP 2,000 (1928); Blackpool Railcoach production batch, GBP 2,356 (1933); Blackpool 249, GBP 2,688 (1934) |
| 1950 | GBP 4,902 body + GBP 2,503 equipment = GBP 7,405 | Blackpool Coronation, Charles Roberts bodies and Crompton Parkinson Vambac equipment |
| 1994 | GBP 1,900,000 | Sheffield Supertram, Siemens-Duewag (stated) |
| 1996-2022 | GBP 1,458,333-8,039,338 per unit | Croydon CR4000 (1996, incl. maintenance) to DLR B23 trainset (2019, incl. support) |

The shape is a step change between the 1930s and the 1990s of roughly three orders of magnitude, not a smooth curve. Within the modern era the series is remarkably flat: every UK tram order from 2007 to 2019 that prices vehicles alone falls between GBP 1.80m and GBP 2.72m per vehicle. The two prices outside that band are the two that bundle a long support obligation into the figure: West Midlands 2019 at GBP 3.98m above it and Croydon 1996 at GBP 1.46m below it, the latter being the oldest order in the modern set and inclusive of a maintenance agreement.

The pakset ladder quoted in the brief (GBP 904 for a 1929 car, GBP 1,976 for 1985, GBP 21,380 for 2012) rises by a factor of about 24 over 1929-2012. The measured nominal series rises by a factor of roughly 900 over 1934-2014. Those cannot both be nominal-price series. Either the pakset ladder is a constant-price series (in which case it needs a deflator and the 1900 anchor of GBP 904 should be checked against the GBP 575-590 measured range, which it exceeds by about 55%), or it is a scaled game-money series whose steps are set by play balance rather than by price history. The research does not resolve which, but it does establish that the modern end of the ladder is not a nominal purchase price and should not be read as one.

Two further observations that bear on the ladder.

- The 1900 anchor is plausible but slightly high. Three independent municipal transactions in 1901-1904 price a standard car at GBP 575-590, and a contemporary engineering estimate puts an electric car at GBP 700. A 1900 value of GBP 904 is at the top of, or a little above, that range; it is not contradicted by the evidence.
- The 1929 anchor looks low. A 1926 Blackpool Standard cost GBP 1,750, a 1928 Pullman GBP 2,000 and a 1934 Balloon GBP 2,688. These are Blackpool cars, and Blackpool was buying premium streamlined vehicles, so they are an upper rather than a typical figure; no source read here prices a plain provincial 1929 car. The gap is a factor of 1.9 against the cheapest of the three and 3.0 against the dearest, so it cannot be dismissed as premium specification alone. The anchor is therefore low by at least a third on the evidence available, but the size of the correction is an open question rather than a finding.

## 7. Dead ends and leads for the next pass

**Dead ends in this pass.**

1. **CoMET/Nova benchmarking.** The TfL Rail and Underground Panel reports for 2015 give maintenance cost per car-km and a breakdown by asset class (rolling stock, infrastructure, station facilities) only as bar charts. The underlying numbers are held by Imperial College's Railway and Transport Strategy Centre and are not in the published appendix. No absolute value for DLR or LU rolling-stock maintenance was obtainable.
2. **Nottingham Express Transit, both fleets.** Line 1 was procured as a GBP 162m fixed-price turnkey contract (Bombardier plus Carillion) covering vehicles, track, power, signalling, depot and highway works, so the 15 Incentro cars have no published price. Phase Two has the same problem: the IRJ sentence "the total value of the LRV contract is £294m, which includes a £101m maintenance package" cannot describe a vehicle contract (it would imply GBP 8.8m per tram). Alstom's own release gives its share as about EUR 350m, including EUR 120m for maintenance, inside a EUR 680m concession that also covers track, power, signalling and depots; the GBP 294m is most likely a currency or transcription error for that share, and in any case it covers infrastructure. Only the GBP 101m maintenance element is usable.
3. **Sheffield Supertram, 1994 fleet and 2015 tram-trains.** The 25 Siemens-Duewag cars were bought inside the GBP 240.6m design-and-build project; the only per-car figure found is the GBP 1.9m asserted in a parliamentary submission. The 7 Vossloh Citylinks sit inside a GBP 58-60m pilot that is mostly electrification, signalling and the Rotherham turnback.
4. **Metrolink and Tyne and Wear maintenance.** Both are whole-system or whole-contract figures (GBP 1.6bn over 11 years; "around £700m" over 35 years) that cannot be decomposed from published material.
5. **Croydon replacement fleet.** The January 2024 contract notice estimates GBP 385m for 24 trams plus spares plus a 30-year technical support and spares agreement; no award has been made, so there is no measured price.
6. **TfL freedom of information.** TfL refused the Croydon Variobahn pricing under section 43(2) (FOI-2321-1718) and refused the DLR B23 bid values under the same exemption (FOI-3107-2526).
7. **South Yorkshire Supertram Business Plan 2024/25.** The PDF is behind an Azure WAF JavaScript challenge; the quoted "around 12%" paragraph came from the search index. The table behind that paragraph, which may separate rolling-stock maintenance from permanent way, needs a browser session to retrieve.
8. **National Tramway Museum collection pages.** The pages that state "cost when new" (Blackpool 167, 249) are readable; the rest of the collection pages generally do not state purchase prices, and the site blocks scripted requests.

**Live leads.**

- **SYMCA new fleet, 2025-2027.** Early market engagement notice of 16 Sep 2025 (2025/S 000-067625) puts the replacement fleet at an estimated GBP 116,000,000 excluding VAT, contract 1 Jul 2027 to 31 Jan 2031, for the 25-tram fleet. Tender notice was expected 19 Dec 2025. This is an ESTIMATE, not a price, but it is the first number for a 2027 UK tram.
- **SYMCA life extension, 2024.** PIN of 22 Aug 2024 (2024/S 000-026935) values the refurbishment of the 25 trams at GBP 13,000,000, i.e. GBP 520,000 per tram. Useful as a mid-life cost, not a purchase price.
- **Croydon, 2024-2026.** Tramtrack Croydon Limited's Tram Replacement Rolling Stock Programme (2024/S 000-000135) for 24 trams plus spares plus a 30-year support agreement, estimated GBP 385m. An award would give the first post-2020 UK tram price.
- **Metrolink next-generation fleet.** TfGM preliminary market engagement published 5-6 Nov 2026, contract estimated 1 Dec 2027 to 30 Nov 2052; no value or vehicle number stated in the notice.
- **Edinburgh.** City of Edinburgh Council Technical Note 3 (11 Mar 2025) states that up to 5 additional trams may be needed for a south-east extension and that a major refurbishment is due within five years. No procurement yet.
- **Nexus Statement of Accounts 2025/26.** Contains a "Fleet Maintenance provision" reclassified to long-term creditors, which may quantify the annual Stadler maintenance charge. Not opened in this pass.
- **DfT LRT0105/LRT0106.** The vehicle-kilometre and vehicle-mile tables are the correct denominators for converting any of the above to per-vehicle-km, and were not loaded.
- **Municipal accounts, 1900-1939.** A car-only historic maintenance figure is the one item that would firm the other side of the era break, and the corporation abstracts of accounts are the most likely place for it (Manchester Transport Department's is catalogued at http://hdl.handle.net/2027/wu.89091473405; Liverpool's annual report at http://hdl.handle.net/2027/wu.89091473363). **Unverified in this pass:** whether a given printed abstract states car repairs and maintenance as a head separate from permanent way. That must be checked against one specific abstract before the head is relied on, because the brief notes that the Ministry of Transport returns combine it. HathiTrust gives search-only access to most of these volumes, so a reading-room or purchased-copy route is needed. The 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica's 1.25d per car-mile for "repairs and maintenance" is a period estimate, not a measured figure, and does not separate cars from track.

## 8. Confidence summary

- **High confidence (measured, contract or final account):** Metrolink 2007 and 2018; Croydon 1996 and 2011 (contract values, both bundling some support or spares); DLR 2005 and 2006 (vehicle-only) and 2019 (inclusive of a fleet support agreement); Edinburgh 2014; Blackpool 2009; West Midlands 2012 (vehicle price) and 2019 (bundled contract value); the Nottingham maintenance element; Wallasey 1901; Dover 1904; Accrington 1906 (combined, no per-car split).
- **Medium confidence (stated by an authoritative party, arithmetic mine):** Metrolink 2008, 2010, 2012, 2013, 2014; Tyne and Wear 2020; Sheffield 1994; Blackpool 1926-1950 series; Sheffield 2024/25 combined maintenance.
- **Low confidence (estimate or qualitative):** Tyne and Wear per-train-year maintenance; the GBP 1.32 per tram-km; any per-seat comparison beyond the two computed points.
