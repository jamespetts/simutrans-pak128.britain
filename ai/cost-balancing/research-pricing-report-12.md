# Research report 12: measured British costs of modern signalling systems

Prepared for pricing 70 modern signal objects and 21 signalboxes in Pak128.Britain-Ex.
All figures are nominal money of the year stated. No deflation has been applied anywhere in
this report or in `research-pricing-rows-12.csv`.

Evidence base: 75 rows in `research-pricing-rows-12.csv`. Domains: 27 signal, 12 signalbox,
36 signalling-system. Every figure was read from a fetched page or a fetched PDF. Where a
figure is a division of a scheme total by a count, the row is marked `derived` and the
arithmetic is shown.

---

## 1. The single most important finding

The modern British measure of signalling cost is the **Signalling Equivalent Unit (SEU)**.
ORR describes a unit as "primarily measured by the number of signalling apparatus and points";
the trade press states the working definition as **one SEU per signal post or per point end**,
regardless of the number of aspects or route indicators. The SEU rate is therefore the closest
thing that exists to an all-in cost per signal position, and it is published in audited
regulatory documents.

| Period | Cost per SEU | Price basis | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 schemes commissioned 1996-2003 | £267,000 | 2003-04 prices | ORR Medium-Term Signalling Review 2005 |
| 2009 (Mod-Sig launch) | £200,000 | current prices | Modern Railways 2019 |
| CP4 average (2009/10-2013/14) | £232,000 | inflation adjusted | ORR 2021 |
| CP5 average (2014/15-2018/19) | £412,000 | inflation adjusted | ORR 2021 |
| CP5 average, restated | £533,000 | 2023-24 prices | ORR 2024 |
| CP6 year 5 | £297,000 | 2023-24 prices | ORR 2024 |
| North Wales Coast, 2018 scheme | £270,000 | 2018-19 prices | Modern Railways 2023 |
| Industry "realistic" estimate | £400,000 | 2019 prices | Modern Railways 2019 |
| Marches line, modelled | £423,000 | 2023 prices | Modern Railways 2023 |
| Target 190plus (digital) | £190,000 | 2017-18 prices | ORR 2021; Arup 2025 |

The CP5 figure of **£412,000 per SEU** is the headline modern per-signal figure. It is
audited, it is stated by the regulator, and it is the number Network Rail itself uses for
planning. The CP4 figure of £232,000 and the 77 per cent rise between CP4 and CP5 are equally
audited and are the reason ORR opened its signalling market study.

Two cautions govern the use of the SEU rate:

1. It is the sum of Network Rail's own costs and the contractor's price. Network Rail told
   ORR that its own costs are "up to around 40%" of a typical re-signalling project's initial
   capital expenditure.
2. It includes interlocking, control, cable, power supply and testing. It is not the price of
   a lineside signal.

### 2.2 The Kent Coast scheme is the best-documented colour-light installation

The IRSE paper by J. F. H. Tyler (read 6 February 1963) contains Appendix E, "Kent Coast
Electrification: Signalling Costs and Data". This is a primary source with the complete cost
and apparatus count for both phases. It supersedes any secondary summary.

| | Phase I | Phase II |
|---|---|---|
| In service | July 1959 | April 1962 |
| Route miles | 97 | 95 |
| Track miles | 230 | 212 |
| Cost | £4.3m | £4.4m |
| Cost per track mile (stated) | £19,000 | £20,750 |
| Single-rail track circuits | 351 | 446 |
| Double-rail track circuits | 579 | 515 |
| Multiple-aspect signals | 361 | 347 |
| Shunt signals | 279 | 233 |
| Point machines | 275 | 447 |
| Cost per running signal (stated) | £12,000 | £12,750 |
| Like-for-like renewal cost | £1.94m | £2.46m |

Derived from those counts:

- Phase I: **£27,545 per route km**, **£11,617 per track km**.
- Phase II: **£28,779 per route km**, **£12,896 per track km**.
- Signalling density: 4.1 running signals per route km and 6.0 track circuits per route km in
  Phase I.

The paper also records the accounting treatment that matters for interpretation: of the £4.3m,
the like-for-like renewal value was £1.94m, and only the difference was treated as new capital
for which a return was demanded. Modern SEU rates have no such split, which is one reason the
two eras cannot be compared directly.

### 2.3 The split between head, post, location case and cable

**No British source was found that gives this split for the 1930s to the 1980s.** This is the
largest structural gap in the evidence.

The two measured components are:

- **Head.** £1,980 per signal head in 2010, Network Rail's weighted average purchase price
  across 33 variants and 1,025 items, from the Arup materials benchmarking study for ORR. This
  is hardware only, ex-works.
- **Cable.** £1.14 to £8.10 per metre in 2010, across five selected variants, from the same
  study. Network Rail's average was 33.2 per cent below the single comparator that supplied
  data.

No price was found for a modern signal post, a location case, or a disconnection box. The 1912
post prices are the only measured post prices in the record.

The consequence for pricing is set out in section 9. In short: measured lineside hardware is a
very small share of the all-in per-signal cost, and the great majority of the SEU rate is
design, installation labour, testing, interlocking, control, power and civils. The 1976 British
Rail Research study of a conventional signalling cost breakdown (IM-SIG-014, RSSB SPARK
pb018930) supports this: for signalling and cable contracts totalling £7.75m, "labour,
overheads and profit represents a major part (43%)", with relay room and power equipment
another major proportion. The full breakdown is behind a login and could not be read.

### 2.4 The 1930s and 1940s

The Southern Railway installed its first colour-light signals in 1926 and had over 900 track
miles of continuous colour-light signalling by 1962 (Tyler 1962). No per-signal price for that
programme was found. The 1939 LMS resignalling of Rugby is recorded as an event, not a cost.

The only 1930s-1940s cost data located is indirect: the York power signalling of 1951 cost
£562,000 for 18 miles of running line, which is £31,200 per route mile or about £19,400 per
route km. That is the earliest whole-scheme power-signalling rate in the record.

## 3. Track circuits

**No measured British price per track circuit was found**, in any era. This is the second
largest gap, and it matters because the brief correctly identifies track circuits as the
dominant cost of a modern scheme.

What the record does contain:

- **Counts.** Kent Coast Phase I had 930 track circuits (351 single-rail, 579 double-rail) in
  230 track miles. Phase II had 961 in 212 track miles. The London, Tilbury and Southend
  resignalling of the early 1960s had 991 track circuits, 422 colour-light signals, 12 hybrid
  boxes and four all-panel boxes over 140 track miles (Tuff, IRSE Proceedings 1962).
- **Whole-scheme cost per track circuit.** £4,624 in Phase I and £4,579 in Phase II. These are
  divisions of the total scheme cost by the number of track circuits, so they are an upper
  bound on the incremental cost: the same money also bought 640 signals and 275 point machines
  in Phase I.
- **Qualitative statements.** Axle counters are "generally more expensive than a single track
  circuit" but can replace several track circuits; modern axle counters are the preferred
  detection method for all new British schemes (Rail Engineer, "Train detection", 2019).
- **A 1988 British Rail Research study** on low-cost axle counters exists (TM-MIC-019) but its
  contents are not public.

The Coded Track Circuits paper (Duckitt, IRSE 1967) and the 1954 Western Region paper discuss
the economics qualitatively — coded track circuits were "proved to be justified on financial
grounds" because they avoided cut sections on 600-yard steady-energy limits — but state no
price.

**Recommendation for track circuits.** Use the Kent Coast derived figure of about £4,600 per
track circuit at 1959-62 prices as a placeholder that includes its share of everything else,
and state the uncertainty. Do not present it as a measured track-circuit price.

### 4.2 Boxes with a date but no cost

The brief named Rugby, Birmingham New Street, Didcot, Three Bridges and Romford. Their dates
are recorded here because the dates are verified even though the costs are not:

- **Rugby PSB** opened 1964, SGE contract, NX panel, controlled the station area south to
  Castlethorpe; closed May 2012.
- **Birmingham New Street PSB**: first phase 9 January 1966, final phase 3 July 1966; replaced
  34 manual boxes; Grade II listed; closed December 2018.
- **Coventry PSB**: commissioned 15 April 1967 (a Rail Engineer article says 1962; the
  chronology says 1967 — the discrepancy is recorded, not resolved).
- **Bescot, Wolverhampton and Walsall**: 1965-66.
- **Saltley**: 1969.
- **Didcot**: the area was controlled from Reading PSB from 1965 and transferred to Swindon B

- **Three Bridges PSB**: opened 25 June 1983, first stage of the resignalling of the
  London-Brighton line under the £130m "Operation New Look" programme. The signalling share of
  that £130m is not stated in the source found.
- **Romford**: no box cost found. Romford is now a ROC.

No cost was found for any of these five boxes individually. That is a real gap: the whole
1960s West Coast Main Line power-box programme (Euston, Watford, Bletchley, Rugby, Coventry,
Birmingham New Street, Wolverhampton, Walsall, Bescot, Saltley) has exactly one priced member,
Euston at £2m.

### 4.3 The ROC programme as a unit-cost event

Network Rail's programme of 12 ROCs is intended to replace more than 800 signal boxes and other
operational locations. At £22m per ROC (Rugby) and 800 boxes divided by 12 ROCs, the capital
cost is about £330,000 of ROC per box displaced. That is a useful ratio for pricing a modern
control centre against a mechanical box: the ROC costs roughly 700 to 1,000 times a 1909
mechanical box, but it replaces 60 to 70 of them.

## 5. CTC and track circuit block

**No British cost per route km for CTC was found.** This is a dead end, and it is worth
explaining why.

The 1956 IRSE paper by O. S. Nock discusses CTC at length and states that "the prohibitive cost
of cable has, up to the present time, largely precluded the bringing of such areas under the
control of one signal box". It gives no price. British Railways proposed a CTC pilot on the
York-Beverley line in 1960; the contract was let and material delivered, but the line closed in
1965 under Beeching and the pilot never ran (Modern Railways 2018). Britain instead adopted
**Track Circuit Block**, which became the formal title in May 1964 and is the British
equivalent of CTC for cost purposes.

The Kent Coast scheme is therefore the best British proxy for "absolute block replaced by track
circuit block with continuous track circuits and colour-light signals":

- £27,545 per route km (Phase I, 1959)
- £28,779 per route km (Phase II, 1962)
- £11,617 and £12,896 per track km respectively

That is the figure to use for a track-circuit-block railway. It is measured, it is a complete
scheme, and it is on the right side of the mechanical-to-power transition.

## 6. ATP, AWS, TPWS and ETCS

### 6.1 ATP, per driving cab

The HSE Research Report 066 figures, quoted in the Crossrail C160 Great Western ATP report
(ORR retains it), give a clean series for one system:

| Year | Cost per cab |
|---|---|
| 1994 | £50,000 |
| 1997 | £70,000 |
| 2002 | £352,000 |
| 2018 (projected) | £550,000 |

The 2002 jump is attributed to obsolescence and monopoly supply, not to added function. A cab,
not a train: a two-cab unit costs twice the figure.

Network-wide ATP was estimated at £1-2bn in 1999 (BBC, quoting the industry), which is why it
was abandoned in favour of TPWS.

### 6.2 AWS

**No per-train or per-signal AWS price was found.** AWS was fitted by British Rail between the
1950s and 1980s; the Cullen/Uff joint inquiry describes the programme but gives no unit cost.
This is a gap. The closest analogue is the TPWS per-train figure, which is a later and more
capable system.

### 6.3 TPWS

The national programme is well documented:

- Total: **£500m**, delivered on time and within budget (Network Rail, 29 December 2003).
- Scope: over 12,000 signals, 650 buffer stops, about 1,000 permanent speed restriction sites,
  and over 6,000 trains.
- Derived: **£36,630 per trackside site**, or £41,667 per signal if the whole sum were spread
  over signals alone. The true per-signal cost is lower because buffer stops and speed
  restriction sites were also fitted.
- An HSE exemption for 12 per cent of the programme saved £64m, which implies a programme
  total of about £533m on the original scope.

The train-borne fitment was funded separately by the train operators, so the £500m should not
be divided by the train count.

### 6.4 ETCS

Four British data points, all nominal:

| Scheme | Year | Cost | Scope | Derived rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cambrian Level 2 | 2011 | £95m outturn | 215 route km, 346 balises, 96 axle counters, 24 DMUs and 3 locomotives | £441,860 per route km; £404,186 trackside per route km |
| Cambrian, contract announcement | 2006 | £60m | first stage | £279,070 per route km (early estimate) |
| Cambrian, contractor's contract value | 2013 | £113m | 2007-2013, design and roll-out | not comparable |
| East Coast Digital Programme | 2024 | £1.4bn | 100 route miles, 1,600 SEUs, about 1,000 signals | £8.70m per route km; £875,000 per SEU; about £4.35m per route km for the infrastructure half |

Additional ETCS facts:

- **In-cab cost per train.** Network Rail told ORR that in-cab equipment "could amount to circa
  £50-60k per train" (2021). The Cambrian outturn was about **£300,000 per Class 158 unit**
  (2011), first-of-class. The gap between the two is the learning curve, and it is a factor of
  five.
- **CP7 funding.** ORR's PR23 final determination allocated **£1,505m** to digital signalling
  across five categories: infrastructure renewals £557m, fleet fitment £699m, enabling
  projects £122m, R&D £68m, CP6 legacy £60m. Fleet fitment is 46 per cent of the total, which
  is the structural difference between ETCS and conventional signalling: the train-borne share
  is large and is funded separately from the infrastructure.
- **ECDP is expensive per route km because it is a dense main line.** 1,600 SEUs in 100 miles
  is 9.9 SEU per route km, against 4.1 running signals per route km on the Kent Coast in 1959.
  The comparison of £8.7m per route km with the Cambrian's £0.44m per route km is therefore
  mostly a comparison of traffic density, not of technology.
- **The Target 190 programme** aims at £190,000 per SEU at 2017-18 prices, down from £315,000.
  It excludes in-cab equipment.

### 6.5 ETCS per balise

No balise unit price was found. The Cambrian had 346 balises in 215 route km, and the scheme
total cannot be apportioned to them meaningfully. Do not derive a per-balise price from the
scheme total.

## 8. Signalbox buildings as distinct from equipment

Only one British source separates the building from the control equipment:

- **Cardiff, 2009**: £16.5m for the signalling and control building, and £3.8m to fit out the
  control room. The fit-out is 19 per cent of the building cost.

Everything else bundles the two. The ROC figures (£15m Derby, £22m Rugby, £26-35m York, £30m
Basingstoke) are design-and-build contracts that include the building, its services, training
facilities and generally the control-room installation.

For a mechanical box, the 1901 Spetisbury figure of £450 covers the box, eight semaphores and
the block instruments, and the 1909 figure of about £300 covers a small box with its signals
and instruments. Neither separates structure from apparatus. The only way to infer a structure
cost is to subtract the signal prices in section 2, which would be an inference, not a
measurement.

### 9.2 Apportioning an all-in rate to a lineside signal object

No British source apportions the SEU rate between the lineside signal and the interlocking,
control and power that serve it. The measured components allow a bounded inference:

- Signal head: £1,980 in 2010, which is 0.5 per cent of a £400,000 signal position.
- Cable: £1.14-£8.10 per metre in 2010. A signal needing 100-200 m of cable therefore carries
  £114-£1,620 of cable.
- Installation labour: 43 per cent of the value of the signalling and cable contracts in the
  1976 British Rail Research study.

A lineside signal object that represents hardware plus its own installation labour therefore
sits well below the all-in rate. An apportionment of **20 to 40 per cent of the all-in rate**
is consistent with the measured hardware prices and the 43 per cent labour share; the
interlocking, control, power, civils and project management take the remainder. This is an
inference, not a measurement, and it should be labelled as such wherever it is used.

## 10. Confidence

**High confidence.** The SEU series from ORR (2003-04, CP4, CP5, 2023-24) is audited and
consistent. The Kent Coast counts and costs are a primary source with the full apparatus
schedule. The TPWS programme total and scope are from Network Rail's completion announcement.
The Cambrian outturn is from a contemporaneous technical account and agrees with two other
sources on route length.

**Medium confidence.** The ECDP figures are programme estimates at different stages, and the
£1.4bn, £1.8bn and £2bn totals cover different scopes. The Jubilee CBTC figure depends on
whether the £170m enabling works are included. The ROC costs are design-and-build contract
values, not outturns, and York's three figures differ.

**Low confidence.** West Hampstead PSB (£8.7m) is a forum recollection. Reading 1963 (£2m) and
Tilbury 1929 (£61,970) are forum-sourced and unverified. The Coventry PSB date conflicts
between sources.

## 11. Conflicts with the knowledge base

Two figures in `New/ai/infrastructure-cost-evidence.md` section 7 do not survive checking
against the primary source. They are recorded here; the knowledge base has not been edited.

1. **"Every running signal cost about £2,272 all in"** on the Kent Coast scheme. The paper
   states £12,000 (Phase I) and £12,750 (Phase II) per running signal. The £2,272 figure is
   reproducible only by dividing the Phase I total by *all* apparatus: 361 multiple-aspect
   signals plus 279 shunt signals plus 930 track circuits plus 275 point machines is 1,845
   items, and £4.3m / 1,845 = £2,331, close to £2,272. The figure is therefore a cost per
   apparatus item, not per running signal, and the label is wrong.

2. **"About £5,200 per route km"** on the same scheme. The measured figure is **£27,545 per
   route km** (Phase I) and £28,779 (Phase II), or £11,617 and £12,896 per track km. £5,200 is
   not reproducible from the source by any division tried.

The direction of the knowledge base's claim survives. The 1912 signal post at £16 13s against
the 1959-62 position at £12,000 is a nominal rise of about 720 times; the mechanical route-km
rate of about £315 against £27,545 is a nominal rise of about 87 times, which after retail
price inflation of roughly four to five times is a real rise of about 20 times. The "15-20
fold real rise" is therefore defensible on the per-route-km basis. It is not defensible on the
per-signal basis, where the nominal rise is much larger.

## 12. Dead ends

Recorded so that they are not searched again.

1. **No British price per track circuit**, in any era. Counts exist, whole-scheme divisions
   exist, unit prices do not.
2. **No British CTC cost per route km.** CTC was rare in Britain; the York-Beverley pilot was
   abandoned in 1965. Track Circuit Block is the British equivalent and is priced through the
   Kent Coast scheme.
3. **No AWS unit price.** The Cullen/Uff inquiry describes the programme without costs.
4. **No per-balise ETCS price.**
5. **No modern signal post or location case price.**
6. **No separate cost for Rugby (1964), Birmingham New Street (1966), Didcot, Three Bridges
   (1983) or Romford signal boxes.** Only Euston and York are priced.
7. **No isolated Victoria line signalling cost.** TfL reports the line upgrade as a whole.
8. **The RSSB SPARK record for the 1976 BR Research cost breakdown (IM-SIG-014) is
   login-gated.** Its abstract gives the £7.75m contract value and the 43 per cent labour
   share but not the appendices.
9. **ORR PDFs now redirect to the ORR homepage.** The 2021 signalling market study and the
   2011 Arup benchmarking study were both retrieved from the Wayback Machine. The live URLs in
   the source column may fail; the archived copies work.
10. **The Historic Hansard search endpoint redirects to Google** and returns no results. The
    modern Hansard API returns divisions rather than debate text for a keyword query.
11. **The Tuff 1962 and Nock 1956 IRSE papers** contain no extractable cost figures: the cost
    tables are images or were not printed.
12. **No 1930s or 1940s colour-light signal price.**
