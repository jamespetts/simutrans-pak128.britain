# Research pass 6 — horses and horse-drawn vehicles: capital, harness, stables and keep components

Date: 2026-11-22. Subagent pass for the pakset cost-balancing project. Outputs: this
report, `research-pricing-rows-6.csv` (171 rows), and
`horse-capital-and-tramway-sources-draft.md`.

Nothing under `New/` was modified. No git commands were run. Currency is nominal
throughout; no deflation and no conversion is applied in the CSV. Where a figure is
derived, the arithmetic is stated in the row notes. Figures that could not be read
cleanly from the page are marked UNVERIFIED with the reason.

The pass was aimed at the two gaps named in the brief: the capital side (only four
capital horse rows existed) and the components of keep.

## 1. Method and searches

One search per turn. Each search result was a lead; the page was fetched and read
before any number was recorded. Three long texts were downloaded and searched
locally rather than read through search summaries: Clark, *Tramways* (1878),
Stephens, *Book of the Farm* (1847 New York edition), and the Commercial Motor
articles.

Searches run, in order:

Two observations follow directly. First, the working horse is not one price class:
in London in 1893 a cab horse at £30 and an omnibus horse at £35 sit alongside
railway cartage horses at £60 and a heavy team horse at 100 guineas. The heavy
draught horse is roughly twice the cab horse. Second, the vestry cart horses of
1890-97 at £47-£70 belong with the railway class, not the omnibus class; they were
bought for heavy two-horse carting, not for speed.

The apparent jump from £28 (Edinburgh, 1874) to £37 16s (North Metropolitan,
1886) is partly a change of animal and partly book value: the Edinburgh figure is a
valuation of a contractor's unsuitable horses, the North Metropolitan figure is the
written-down book value of horses being discarded.

### 2.2 The Fulham vestry list: 30 individual purchases, 1881-1890

The single best new capital source is a one-page table in the Fulham Medical
Officer's report, "Horses and Fodder: Particulars of Horses belonging to Vestry of
Fulham" (p. 156; Wellcome table 10199536001700155). It lists 30 horses by colour,
year of purchase, cost and present age. The purchases run from 1881 to 1890:

- 1881: £70 (bay, present age 16).
- 1884: £65 (chestnut, age 13).
- 1885: £35 ("pay horse", age unknown).
- 1888: £52 10s; £10 (marked "Exchange", so not a market price); £55.
- 1889: £55, £58, £30.
- 1890: twenty horses at £47, £48, £55 (four), £57, £58, £59, £60 (ten),
  £62 10s.

The 1890 cluster is tight: 17 of the 20 are £55-£60. The two cheap animals (£30 in
1889, £35 in 1885) are the exceptions that prove the rule. Reading the present ages
against the purchase years implies purchase at about five to seven years old and
service to about 16, i.e. up to ten or eleven years in a vestry stud. That reading
is **[computed]** from the two columns and is not stated by the report.

The same report's hire comparison gives the stock values used for its 15 per cent
replacement allowance: £1,000 for 21 horses in 1890 (£47 12s 4d each) and £1,800
for 32 horses in 1891 (£56 5s each). It also states the working year used in the
comparison: **310 days per annum**.

### 2.3 Omnibus, cab and carrier horses, 1893

Gordon's *Horse World of London* supplies a complete capital structure for the
London horse trades, which the existing KB used only for keep:

- LGOC omnibus horses: bought at five, £35, work five years, sold for £5.
- Road Car Company: about £35 each, carcass value about 30s.
- Cab horses: Irish four-year-olds at £30, last three years, sold for £9 into a
  tradesman's cart. A good hansom might have a 50-guinea horse.
- Mail horses: £36, six years in the service.
- Railway cartage horses: 6,000 in London, "not over-valuing at £60 apiece";
  the heaviest team horses at 100 guineas; Midland cast horses average £10 and
  Brighton £12 6s.
- The whole London carrying trade: 19,000 horses bought for close on £900,000
  (£47 7s 4d each); with railways, 25,000 horses worth £1,260,000 (£50 8s each).

The resale ratios are the useful part: cab 30 per cent of purchase price after
three years, omnibus 14 per cent after five, tram 16 per cent after four, railway
17-21 per cent after four to seven, vestry 52 per cent after an unknown period.
Intensity of work, not age alone, sets the residual value.

### 2.4 Horse tramways

Clark's *Tramways* (1878) is a new source family for the pakset's tram objects. It
reproduces company accounts and gives:

- North Metropolitan: horse power hired from the LGOC at **6½d per car-mile**,
  including keep, keepers' wages, stable rent, harness and renewal; 11 horses per
  car, car doing 70 miles a day, each pair 14-16 miles a day (about 6½ miles per
  horse per day). Horses start at five and last four years on tram work.
- Edinburgh: the company bought its contractor's horses at a valuation of **£28**
  in 1874.
- Dublin: 76 cars at **£230 each**; harness, stable implements, forges and tools
  £1,000 for 486 horses and 76 cars (£2 1s 6d per horse).
- Liverpool: the accounts valued each horse at **£30** in 1877, which a shareholder
  attacked as too high for the "dilapidated and jaded hacks" on Scotland Road.
- North Metropolitan 1886: 111 horses discarded in a half year had cost
  £4,199 10s (£37 16s 8d each) and fetched £667 11s 6d (£6 0s 2d each); 57 died,
  yielding £102 in carcasses.

The contract rate of 6½d per car-mile is directly comparable with the pakset's
running-cost unit and is the most valuable single tram figure found. It is a
package rate, so it must not be added to separate keep components.

### 2.5 Military remounts, 1887-1909

Hansard gives a clean official series:

- 1887: heavy cavalry £42, light cavalry £40, artillery £45; ages four to six.
- 1895: the War Office would give £40 for a horse.
- 1900: cavalry £45-£50; mounted infantry cobs £30; English horses £35-£40,
  Australian £45 and Argentine £26 delivered in South Africa (passage £35).
- 1902: Hungarian contracts at £35 (artillery), £30 (cavalry) and £20 (cobs);
  open-market Hungarian cobs £8-£12; the Austrian Government paid £28.
- 1909: cobs £30, troop horses £42, chargers £60.

The 1902 debate is also a warning about contract prices: the same cobs were bought
at £33 16s 8d, £26 and £20 in successive contracts, and the open market price was
£8-£12.

### 2.6 The Commercial Motor cost tables, 1909-1922

These are trade-press costings, not company accounts, but they itemise the same
components as the vestry reports and extend the series past 1900:

- **1909**, a laundryman's statement: horses £30 each, vans £30 each, harness £10
  a set; shoeing £4 4s a horse-year, veterinary £1, harness repairs £1 a set,
  stabling £5, fodder and bedding 16s a week (£41 12s a year).
- **1920**, "The Costly Horse": a 10 cwt one-horse van outfit is a £70 horse, a
  £70 van and £10 of harness and gear; a two-horse 30 cwt van has £80 horses, an
  £80 van and £15 of harness; a five-ton dray has £100-£140 horses and at least
  £150 of dray and harness. Fodder is 41s a week (oats 21s, hay 13s, chaff 4-5s,
  bran and sundries 2s 6d). The article depreciates the outfit over seven years
  and charges interest at 6 per cent.
- **1922**, farm cart comparison: a £75 horse, a £65 wagon, food 15s a week
  (£40 a year), shoeing £5, veterinary £5, stabling £5, harness depreciation and
  repairs £3 10s, depreciation of the horse £9 7s 6d; 40 working weeks a year.

The 1922 figures are internally consistent: the components sum to £67 17s 6d,
which the article divides by 40 weeks to get £1 14s a week. The OCR is poor
(£ appears as 2 and 5 as 25), so the values are reconstructed from context and from
that total and are marked UNVERIFIED.

### 2.8 Vehicle capital prices

The 1862 London Exhibition catalogues, transcribed by Grace's Guide, give two
manufacturers' net price lists in the same year. Rock and Son (carriages, in
guineas, one-horse column): single brougham 120, double brougham 130, sociable
landau 160, dioropha landau 190, sociable coach-landau-barouche 210, open sociable
130, enclosed sociable 145, waggonette 65, dog cart from 30, victoria phaeton
55-90, pony phaeton from 25. George Ell (commercial vehicles, in pounds): pillar
van 50 cwt £40-46, builder's van £40-60, railway or carrier's van £36-60,
furniture van £45-55, agricultural cart £11-18, agricultural waggon £30-40,
improved dray £25-45, two-horse brick cart £20-30, one-horse brick cart £13-21,
light spring cart £16-26, stone truck £35-50, timber carriage £28-45.

Later trade lists fill the gap to 1900:

- Thomas King, Berkhamsted (account books 1840s-1880s, via Herts Memories): a
  one-horse landau £180 (1874), a phaeton £107, a grocer's van £20, a manure cart
  £10, a pony cart £16, a covered bread barrow £7.
- Stokes and Son, King's Cliffe, 1890 catalogue: landau from 100 guineas, brougham
  from 70 guineas, governess car £23, estate wagon £29 10s.
- J & R Chapman, King's Cliffe, from 1896: wagon £30 single-shafted, £40
  double-shafted, farm cart £12.
- Gordon 1893: hansom 100 guineas; London omnibus £150 average; Road Car road car
  £160; the 1760 Manchester stage coach £100 (Gerhold).
- Clark 1878: Dublin tramcar £230.

The private-carriage range is wide because size and finish dominate: a provincial
brougham at 70 guineas in 1890 sits below a London brougham at 120-140 guineas in
1862. Any pakset use must match the type and the finish, not just the name.

### 2.9 Stables and mews

Two London vestry accounts give a building cost per horse that is far above
Gordon's London-wide figures:

- Battersea 1890: stables at Bridge Road West £1,048 7s 4d, new harness room £64,
  stables at Battersea Park Road £402 1s 5d = £1,514 8s 9d for an average of 23.8
  horses working, or **£63 12s 7d per horse** [computed].
- Wandsworth 1894: first cost of stables £700 for 11 cart horses = **£63 12s 8d
  per horse**; fitting up loose boxes £31 16s.
- Wandsworth 1896: buildings £811 4s 10d for 14.05 horses = **£57 14s 10d per
  horse**.
- Fulham 1890-91: rent £199 17s 4d for stables with room for 44 = **£4 10s 10d
  per stall-year**.
- Gordon 1893: cab stable accommodation, fittings, plant and working cash
  £170,000 for 15,000 horses = £11 6s 8d per horse; LGOC and Road Car stables
  £200,000 for about 13,000 horses = £15 7s 8d per horse.

The two vestry figures agreeing at £63 is striking, and it is the best evidence
found for the capital cost of an urban horse stall. Gordon's £11-£15 is a
depreciated or partial valuation, not a building cost, and should not be used for
new construction.

### 2.10 Working life, working days and work done

| Source | Life or work |
|---|---|
| Clark 1878 (North Metropolitan) | horses start at 5; tram life 4 years; 11 horses per car; 70 miles a car-day; 6½ miles a horse-day |
| Gordon 1893 | omnibus 5 years, tram 4, cab 3, mail 6, railway 4-7; omnibus horse 12 miles a day |
| Fulham 1890-91 | 310 working days a year used in the hire comparison |
| Battersea 1890 | 23.8 horses working daily after deducting Sundays, holidays and sick |
| Wandsworth 1894 | 11 cart horses; stables for the parish |
| Norfolk 1919 (existing) | 267 days worked |
| Commercial Motor 1922 | 40 working weeks a year |

The 310-day figure is the best direct statement for a two-horse cart stud; 267 days
is a farm figure including weather losses; 40 weeks is a costing convention. They
are not contradictory: 310 days is about 51 weeks of six days, 267 is about 44
weeks, 40 weeks is 240 days.

### 2.11 Keep components

New complete or near-complete accounts:

- **Paddington 1878**: keep and bedding 19s 9.59d per horse-week, averaged over
  three tenders, with hay at 2s 8.66d a truss, clover 3s 6.66d, straw 1s 5.33d,
  oats 2s 9.75d a bushel and bran 4.87d. This is higher than Paddington's 1892
  figure (16s 3d) and fits the pre-1879 price level.
- **Battersea 1890** (23.8 horses): provender £35 7s 8d, shoeing £3 3s 5d,
  veterinary £1 8s 2d, harness repairs £1 9s, horsekeeper and stable help
  £6 9s 4d per horse-year.
- **Wandsworth 1894** (11 horses): provender £35 17s 11d, shoeing £2 15s 11d,
  veterinary £1 2s 5d, harness hire £2 4s 5d, horsekeeper £6 4s 5d.
- **Wandsworth 1896** (14.05 horses): provender £34 18s 6d, shoeing £3 6s 8d,
  veterinary £1 4s 8d, horsekeeper £5 18s 7d.

The component structure is stable across the three vestry accounts: feed about
£35 a horse-year, shoeing £2 15s to £3 6s, veterinary £1 2s to £1 8s, harness
£1 9s to £2 4s, wages £5 18s to £6 9s. This confirms the existing KB finding that
the ratios between components are stable, and it extends the series to 1878-1896
with three additional establishments.

## 4. Dead ends and things not found

- **Carriage duty returns do not list vehicle values.** The duty was assessed by
  the number of horses and wheels, and the returns used by the *Spectator* in 1877
  give counts of carriages, not values. Gordon gives the London cab duty as 15s
  (plus a £2 police licence). No value series exists in the duty records as
  described in the brief. Recorded as a dead end, not a gap to fill.
- **No railway company horse account was found.** Gordon gives stock values,
  resale prices and working life for the London railway studs, but not the annual
  horse account. Clark's LGOC abstract gives only a combined stock-in-trade figure
  of £522,116 for "omnibuses, horses, harness, goodwill and appurtenances", which
  cannot be split.
- **No hunter price.** The only hunter figure is a 1790s "good hunter or coach
  horse" at 30 guineas. Later hunter prices are bloodstock sales (Crabbet Arab
  stud, average £120), which are not working hunter prices.
- **No mule or donkey price** for Britain was found. The 1900 Hansard debate
  mentions mules shipped from Naples but gives no price.
- **No omnibus price before 1893** beyond the 1760 stage coach. The 1830 stage
  coach figure (£130-£150) is a forum quotation of an unidentified archived
  source and is marked UNVERIFIED.
- **No four-wheeler cab price.** Gordon says only that it cost less than a hansom.
- **The Morgan & Co. catalogue (c.1893-1901)** is described by an auction house as
  having a printed price table, but no prices are visible in the listing. Lead
  only.
- **The Barker & Co. account book (1830-1897)** at City of Westminster Archives
  and the **S. Hammond estimate book (1874-77)** at Senate House are not digitised.
  Archival leads, not usable in this pass.
- **Stephens, *Book of the Farm***: the 1847 New York edition has no usable
  capital prices; it gives only "cost of one horse each day, 11½d". The 1844
  Edinburgh edition was not read page by page.
- **Pollins, "British horse tramway company accounting practices"** (1991) is
  paywalled; only the abstract was read.
- **Paddington 1878 "average cost of £80 each"** could not be attached with
  certainty to horses rather than carts, because the page begins mid-paragraph.
  It is therefore not recorded as a horse price.

## 5. Most valuable points

1. **The type differential is real and large.** In 1893 London, a cab horse cost
   £30, an omnibus horse £35, a mail horse £36, a railway cartage horse £60 and a
   heavy team horse 100 guineas. A single "horse price" for the pakset would be
   wrong by a factor of three.
2. **The vestry cart horse is the best-documented 1890s heavy horse:** 30 horses
   listed with 28 cash prices at £47-£70, a stock value of £56-£63, and two
   independent stable building costs of about £63 a stall.
3. **The resale ratios** (cab 30 per cent, omnibus 14, tram 16, railway 17-21,
   vestry 52) are a direct measure of how hard each service worked the animal and
   can inform depreciation in the pakset model.
4. **Contract horsing at 6½d per car-mile** (North Metropolitan, 1871-76) is the
   cleanest all-in horse-power rate found and is directly comparable with a
   vehicle running cost.
5. **The component structure is stable** across Battersea 1890, Wandsworth 1894
   and 1896: feed about £35, shoeing £2 15s-£3 6s, veterinary £1 2s-£1 8s,
   harness £1 9s-£2 4s, wages £5 18s-£6 9s per horse-year. This is stronger
   evidence than any single total.
6. **310 working days a year** for a vestry cart stud is a direct, stated figure
   that sits between the 267-day farm year and the 40-week costing convention.
