# Research pass 51 - Mainline carriage maintenance, 1920-1990

Research pass for the Pak128.Britain-Ex cost balancing. Compiled 25 November 2026.
Files written: this report and `research-pricing-rows-51.csv`. No workbook, `.dat` file or
existing document was changed.

This pass tests the carriage maintenance ladder that [report 43](research-pricing-report-43.md)
fixed at 33 / 140 / 265 / 460 / 440 GBP per vehicle-year in 1900 money at 1900 / 1950 / 1970 /
1990 / 2020. A dated point in such a series is called a **rung** below, following reports 43 and
44. Only the 1900 rung is measured; the others are interpolations or derivations. The pass looks
for measured carriage maintenance at or near 1920, 1948, 1966 and 1990 so that the ladder can be
tested against accounts rather than against a smooth line drawn between two measured points.

## 1. What this pass found

1. **Five British railway companies measured their own carriage upkeep in 1912, and the figures lie
   either side of Hill's 1892 level.** A Commons debate on the Taff Vale Railway Bill on 11 March
   1912 quotes the companies' comparison: Taff Vale **£34.83 a carriage-year**, Barry **£34.43**,
   Brecon and Merthyr **£23.29**, Cambrian **£18.73**, Rhymney **£33.91**. All five worked in
   South Wales, and the Cambrian also worked into England. The speaker supposes the figure
   "includes cleaning" and does not say whether renewals are inside it. **MEASURED** (company
   figures quoted in Parliament). In 1900 money at the general index 108 the five are
   £32.25 / £31.88 / £21.56 / £17.34 / £31.40, mean £26.89. Hill's 1892 gross figure is £34.3 in
   1900 money. The real level of carriage upkeep was therefore flat, or falling slightly, over
   the twenty years to 1912, and it was not growing.

2. **The Ministry of Transport estimated the account line for all rolling-stock maintenance either
   side of the First World War, and it rose only 14 per cent in real terms.** The *Railway
   Magazine* of 1920 reports Sir J. G. Beharrell, Director-General of the Finance and Statistics
   Section of the Ministry of Transport, preparing train-mile costs for the Rates Advisory
   Committee for 1920-21. "Maintenance and renewal of rolling-stock is estimated at £40,600,000
   (including £600,000 for the Government pool arrangement)... against a total of £13,823,094 in
   1913." That line, called the rolling-stock head below, covers locomotives, carriages and
   wagons together.
   The same article gives maintenance of way and works as £35,000,000 against £12,562,165 in
   1913, and locomotive running as £57,000,000 against £18,166,048. **MEASURED** (an official
   estimate published in a contemporary journal; the passenger and freight halves of the
   rolling-stock figure, £19,076,000 and £21,524,000, sum exactly to £40,600,000, which shows
   the figures are transcribed correctly). At the general index, 107 in 1913 and 275 in 1920,
   the real change is **×1.143**, or ×1.126 if the £600,000 pool item is excluded.

3. **That measurement is consistent with an independent parliamentary statement.** On 17 July
   1922 the Parliamentary Secretary to the Ministry of Transport told the House that "the sums
   charged in respect of maintenance of way and works and rolling stock were £25,000,000 in 1913
   and £86,000,000 in 1921. In the latter year certain companies were overtaking arrears of
   maintenance." The 1913 figure cross-checks the *Railway Magazine* pair: £12.56m of way and
   works plus £13.82m of rolling stock is £26.4m, against the £25m given in the House. The 1921
   figure is 3.44 times 1913 against a price index ratio of 2.35, and it covers a year in which
   arrears were being made up. **MEASURED.**

4. **The 1920 rung of the ladder, 68, has no measured support and asks for carriage work to have
   risen about twice as fast as the rest of the fleet.** 68 is 1.98 times the 1892 level of 34.3.
   The measured real rise in the rolling-stock total between 1913 and 1920-21 was 1.14. Carriages
   were only about a tenth of that total (finding 6), so the total cannot refute a doubling of
   carriage work on its own; a doubling of the carriage element would raise the total by about
   10 per cent, which is inside the measured 14 per cent. What the total does show is that there
   was no general real increase in rolling-stock maintenance in 1920-21 large enough to suggest
   that carriage work doubled. **DERIVED.**

5. **The measured 1912 level, carried forward at the measured 1913-1921 real change, gives about
   £39 a carriage-year in 1900 money for 1920, not 68.** The chain is: 1912 mean £29.04 nominal
   (£26.89 in 1900 money at index 108) × 1.143 = £30.7 if the 1912 figure is gross of renewals, or
   £47.3 if it is a repairs-only figure grossed up by the 35 per cent renewals share. The midpoint
   is **£39**, band £28-55. The 1912 figure is deflated at index 108 and the real change is
   measured from 1913 at index 107; the 0.9 per cent difference between the two index values is
   well inside the band. **DERIVED**, with the renewals treatment of the 1912 figure as the
   dominant uncertainty.

6. **Carriages were about a tenth of the rolling-stock total in 1913, which is why the total is a
   weak instrument.** At Hill's 1892 £33.3 a carriage-year and roughly 40,000 carriages, the
   carriage element was about £1.3m at 1892 prices, or £1.5m at 1913 prices, against the measured
   £13.8m total: **9-11 per cent**. Wagons at £4.2 over roughly 700,000 vehicles contributed about
   £2.9m at 1892 prices, and locomotives the remaining £9m or so. The split is an arithmetic
   reconstruction, not a measured return, and it is the reason finding 4 is stated as "cannot
   refute" rather than "refutes". **DERIVED.**

7. **The fleet denominators for 1938, 1948 and 1955 are measured and published.** British
   Railways' own booklet gives passenger-carrying vehicles (locomotive-hauled, multiple-unit and
   railcars) as **43,197 at 1938, 40,351 at 1948 and 41,715 at 1955**, with seats and berths.
   The same booklet gives 1955 working expenses with "maintenance and depreciation of rolling
   stock" at **£94.0m**, which is all classes and includes depreciation and so cannot be divided
   by the carriage count. **MEASURED.**

8. **The statutory class-level carriage account did not exist for 1920.** On 6 December 1920 the
   Minister of Transport told the House that the detailed division of expenditure between
   companies "ceased to be necessary when the Government guaranteed to the companies their
   aggregate net receipts of the year 1913, and it was accordingly suspended to save labour and
   expense. It is therefore not practicable for individual companies to publish detailed
   particulars of the present cost of working of their several departments." The 1911 Act
   Abstract B, which separates "Complete renewals", "Repairs and partial renewals" and "Workshop
   expenses" for carriages, was therefore not published for the control years. That is the
   structural reason no measured 1920 carriage figure exists, and it is why the pass reaches
   the 1921-22 returns instead. **MEASURED** (ministerial statement).

9. **1948, 1966 and 1990 were not closed.** No published class-level carriage maintenance figure
   for those years was found in a readable source. The accounts that hold them are identified in
   section 7 with the table to request. **UNAVAILABLE.**

## 2. Method

- **Deflator.** Every nominal figure is converted with the `general` series in
  `New/config/prices.tab` (1900 = 100): 1892 97, 1912 108, 1913 107, 1920 275, 1921 251,
  1938 183, 1948 338, 1950 359, 1955 468, 1961 552, 1966 660, 1970 795, 1990 5,408, 2020 12,521.
  `prices.tab` states "Vehicle maintenance stays on the general index. No UK maintenance or repair
  price index exists before January 1987", so maintenance figures use `general` and the
  vehicle-purchase series is not used anywhere in this pass. This is the same convention as
  [report 43](research-pricing-report-43.md), [report 44](research-pricing-report-44.md) and
  [report 47](research-pricing-report-47.md).
- **Grades.** MEASURED = an account, return, contract or published total with a stated scope, or
  an official estimate published as such. STATED = a trade, academic or witness statement of a
  rate without the underlying account. DERIVED = arithmetic from measured or stated figures with
  the chain shown. ESTIMATE = judgement, used only where nothing measured exists and marked in
  every table. UNAVAILABLE = identified but not readable.
- **Definitions.**
  - **Carriage maintenance** is the money spent in a year on keeping passenger-carrying railway
    vehicles in service: superintendence, complete renewals charged to revenue, repairs and
    partial renewals, and carriage-workshop expenses. It excludes locomotive running, train
    working, heating fuel, lighting, cleaning, catering and capital expenditure on new vehicles
    except where a source says otherwise.
  - **Gross** means the figure as printed, with revenue renewals inside it. **Net** means the
    figure less an estimated renewals provision. A source that says "repairs and renewals"
    contains renewals; a source that says "repairs" does not.
  - **A carriage-year** is one passenger-carrying vehicle in stock for one year. It is not a
    vehicle-mile and not a train-mile. Where a source gives train-miles, the conversion needs the
    mean formation, and that assumption is stated wherever it is used.
  - **A works overhaul** is a periodic visit to a railway workshop at which the vehicle is
    stripped, its running gear, bogies and brake gear are attended and the body is repaired and
    repainted. It is charged to revenue maintenance, unlike a rebuild or a life extension, which
    renew the structure and are capital.
  - **The reference duty** is the assumed mean annual distance in kilometres used to convert
    between a per-year figure and a per-kilometre figure. It is an assumption, not a measurement,
    wherever the source does not state the fleet's annual mileage.
  - **The rolling-stock head** is the account line "maintenance and renewal of rolling stock",
    which covers locomotives, carriages and wagons together. It is not a carriage figure and is
    used here only as a constraint on the carriage figure, with the share stated.
- **Scope of "mainline carriage".** The 1912 figures are company averages over all passenger
  carriages, four- and six-wheeled and bogie, main-line and suburban, because no company
  published a figure by type. The British Railways figures are all passenger-carrying vehicles
  including multiple-unit cars and railcars. No source read in this pass gives a cost per vehicle
  type (non-corridor, corridor, bogie, electric multiple unit) for any of the four dates; the
  types are discussed in section 7 as a gap.
- **Sourcing.** Every page relied on was fetched and read. The *Railway Magazine* volume was read
  from the Internet Archive text layer of the 1920 volume and the figures were checked by adding
  the passenger and freight halves to the stated total. The Hansard pages were read from the
  Historic Hansard text. The British Railways booklet was read from the scanned PDF. Where a
  source was identified but could not be read (HathiTrust, lending-restricted periodicals,
  Google Books), it is graded UNAVAILABLE and named in section 7.
- **No `.dat` value and no workbook figure is used as evidence anywhere in this pass.** The pakset
  capital ladders do not appear at all.

## 4. The series and the comparison with the 33/140/265/460/440 ladder

### 4.1 What the new evidence says about each rung

| Rung | Ladder value | Nearest measured evidence | Test result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1900 | 33 | Hill 1892: £33.3 nominal gross, £34.3 in 1900 money (measured) | Confirmed. The 1912 company figures, mean £26.89 in 1900 money, sit just below it, so the 1900 rung is if anything a little high rather than low |
| 1920 | 68 | 1912 company mean £26.89; 1913-1921 rolling-stock head x1.143 real | **Not supported.** A carriage figure consistent with both measurements is £30.7-£47.3, midpoint £39. 68 requires carriage work to have risen about 1.7 times faster than the rest of the fleet between 1913 and 1921, with no measurement saying it did |
| 1948 | (interpolated 134) | Fleet count 40,351 measured; no carriage cost | Re-drawn to £112 from the revised 1920 rung; the point stays an interpolation |
| 1950 | 140 | No carriage cost | **Re-drawn to 121** by log-linear interpolation between 39 at 1920 and the measured 183 at 1961 |
| 1966 | (interpolated 231) | No carriage cost | Re-drawn to 226 from 121 at 1950 to 265 at 1970; the movement is small |
| 1970 | 265 | No new evidence | Unchanged |
| 1990 | 460 | No new evidence | Unchanged |

### 4.2 The shape the ladder then has

In 1900 money per vehicle-year: 33 (1900), 39 (1920), 112 (1948), 121 (1950), 183 (1961),
226 (1966), 265 (1970), 460 (1990), 440 (2020).

In the money of the day the same rungs are about £107 (1920), £379 (1948), £434 (1950), £1,010
(1961), £1,492 (1966), £2,107 (1970), £24,877 (1990) and £55,092 (2020). The 1961 nominal figure
is a restatement of the £1,012 a coach-year derived in [report 43](research-pricing-report-43.md)
section 4 at the index 552; it checks the arithmetic, not the measurement.

The real growth rates between the rungs are:

| Interval | Growth | Per year |
|---|---|---|
| 1892-1920 | x1.14 | 0.5 per cent |
| 1920-1950 | x3.10 | 3.9 per cent |
| 1950-1970 | x2.19 | 4.0 per cent |
| 1970-1990 | x1.74 | 2.8 per cent |
| 1990-2020 | x0.96 | flat |

The existing ladder implies 2.5 per cent a year from 1892 to 1950 and 3.2 per cent from 1950 to
1970. The revised shape moves the growth from the 1892-1920 stretch, where the measurements say
there was almost none, into the 1920-1970 stretch. A steady 4 per cent a year there is consistent
with rising real wages in a labour-intensive activity and with the change in the stock from
timber-bodied four- and six-wheelers to steel-bodied, electrically lit and steam-heated bogie
vehicles; that consistency is a judgement, not a measurement.

### 4.3 Why the 1920 test is weaker than it looks

The rolling-stock total is dominated by locomotives and wagons. A change in one tenth of such a
total is nearly invisible: if carriage maintenance had doubled between 1913 and 1921 while the
rest of the fleet stood still in real terms, the measured total would have risen by about
10 per cent, against the 14 per cent actually measured. The total therefore cannot separate
"carriages doubled and the rest fell slightly" from "everything rose about 14 per cent". The pass
records the 1920 rung as unsupported and recommends the lower reading, but it does not claim that
the total proves 68 wrong. The document that would settle it is a class split of the 1921 or 1922
Abstract B, which exists (section 7).

## 5. Recommended treatment

1. **Change the 1920 rung from 68 to 39** in `CARRIAGE_MAINT_LADDER` in
   `New/ai/cost-balancing/evidence_data.py` (line 877 at this commit), keeping the 1900 rung at 33
   and reading the series log-linearly between the points and flat outside them. Record the grade
   as DERIVED with band 28-55, not ESTIMATE, because the chain runs from two measured figures (the
   1912 company mean and the 1913-1921 official aggregate) with a named assumption between them.
2. **Re-draw the 1950 rung from 140 to 121** and the 1948 point from 134 to 112, both by
   log-linear interpolation between 39 at 1920 and the measured 183 at 1961. This is the same
   interpolation method the ladder already uses; only its left-hand anchor changes. The
   replacement tuple is `((1900, 33.0), (1920, 39.0), (1950, 121.0), (1970, 265.0),
   (1990, 460.0), (2020, 440.0))`; the 1948 and 1966 values are then read from the interpolation
   and are not separate entries.
3. **Leave 1970, 1990 and 2020 alone.** No new measurement touches them, and the 1966 point moves
   only from 231 to 226 as a consequence of the 1950 change.
4. **Carry the change through the overhaul table.** The overhaul provision in
   [maintenance-decomposition](../vehicle-cost/maintenance-decomposition.md) "Carriage overhaul by
   era" and in [report 44](research-pricing-report-44.md) section 5 is a share of A, the annual
   total. For the 1890-1922 band the event is 1.20 x A: at A = 39 it is **£47**, against £82 at
   A = 68. The interval stays at 180,000 km, which is five years at the band's reference duty of
   36,000 km a year in [report 44](research-pricing-report-44.md) section 5; that duty is an
   assumption for 1920 and is not measured. The 24 per cent provision and the renewals deduction
   are unchanged. The envelope rule — the rule that the components the engine charges must sum to
   the measured total, with no component charged twice and none omitted — is unaffected, because
   the provision is still carved out of A; only A moves.
5. **Do not apply the change to the 183 anchor at 1961.** It is a measured figure with its own
   composition (capital provision inside the Beeching functional line, per
   [report 43](research-pricing-report-43.md) section 4) and it is not re-opened here.
6. **Record the composition on the row.** The 1912 figures are "upkeep of carriages" with cleaning
   probably inside and renewals unstated; the 1913 and 1920-21 figures are "maintenance and
   renewal of rolling stock", gross of renewals and covering all classes. The derivation assumes
   that the 1912 figure is comparable with the gross head; that assumption is the weakest link
   and is why the band is wide.
7. **Do not enter a new per-vehicle-type scale.** No source read in this pass prices a
   non-corridor, corridor, bogie or electric multiple-unit carriage separately for any of the
   four dates. The class columns stay on the existing rules.

| 1948 carriage rung | 112 | 85-137 | As 1950 |

**Structural assumptions.** (i) The 1912 figures and the 1913 head describe the same activity; if
the company "upkeep" figure excludes renewals and workshop expenses, the 1912 level is
understated and the 1920 rung moves up. (ii) The carriage element moved with the rolling-stock
head between 1913 and 1921; the whole width of the 1920 band is the price of that assumption.
(iii) The 1961 anchor is a maintenance-plus-capital-provision figure, so the growth rate from
1950 to 1961 is overstated by whatever the capital provision is. (iv) The 1912 sample is five
companies, not a national average; the national level could differ in either direction.

## 7. Gaps and routes

- **The class split of the 1921 or 1922 Abstract B.** The 1911 Act form separates "Complete
  renewals", "Repairs and partial renewals" and "Workshop expenses" for carriages, wagons and
  locomotives separately. HathiTrust's catalogue record for the *Railway Returns* (record
  100007368) lists full view for 1923-1930, but the pages could not be read in this pass because
  the site blocks automated access. The 1921 and 1922 returns are Parliamentary papers; the
  British Library holds the set and the nationalised boards' predecessors' records are at The
  National Archives, Kew. **Request:** the all-companies summary of Abstract B for 1921 and 1922,
  carriage column, with the coaching-vehicle count. That single table would replace the derived
  1920 rung with a measured one. **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **1948 carriage maintenance.** The British Transport Commission's first *Annual Report and
  Accounts*, for 1948 (HMSO, 1949; not seen in this pass), carries the consolidated revenue
  account and the statistical appendix; the Railway Executive's own accounts carry the
  departmental detail. **Request:** the "maintenance and renewals of rolling stock" head split
  between locomotives, carriages and wagons, and the passenger-carrying vehicle count at
  31 December 1948. The TUC Library Collections at London Metropolitan University hold the
  British Railways Board annual report run (classmark HE 3015,
  https://tuclibrary.maxarchiveservices.co.uk/index_cards/british-railways-board-2/), and the
  nationalised boards' records are at The National Archives, Kew. **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **1966 carriage maintenance.** The British Railways Board's *Annual Report and Accounts 1966*
  (HMSO, 1967; not seen in this pass) carries the "maintenance of rolling stock" line and the
  rolling-stock statement. The Board's reports number their tables (Hansard, 15 December 1980,
  vol. 996, c. 70W, cites "tables 3A and 3B, page 52" and "table 5M, page 77" of the 1979
  report), so the request can name a table once a copy is in hand. **Request:** the rolling-stock
  maintenance note and the vehicle statement for 1966, with the locomotive-hauled coach count.
  **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **1990 carriage maintenance.** The British Railways Board's *Annual Report and Accounts
  1990/91* (not seen in this pass) and, from 1992, the sector accounts. **Request:** the
  rolling-stock maintenance line and the locomotive-hauled coach count, and the split between
  locomotive-hauled coaches, multiple units and freight vehicles if the note carries one.
  **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **A cost per vehicle type.** No source read in this pass gives non-corridor, corridor, bogie or
  electric multiple-unit carriage maintenance separately for any date. The likely holders are the
  BRB's cost-of-ownership data bank (established after the Serpell report, section 6.42), the
  regional chief mechanical engineers' annual repair-cost returns, and the carriage-works
  job-cost records. **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **The mean annual mileage for 1920, 1948 and 1966.** The ladder divides by a reference duty, not
  a measured one, for these years. Report 44 measures 29,000-41,000 km a carriage-year for 1892
  and 66,000-110,000 km for 1961. The 1920 and 1948 figures would come from the same Railway
  Returns vehicle-mile tables; without them the per-km part of the split is an assumption.
  **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **A carriage works-overhaul price for 1920 or 1948.** Report 44 has events for the 1860s and for
  1982 only. A 1920 or 1948 works order would test the 24 per cent provision in the 1890-1922
  band, which this pass leaves untouched. **UNAVAILABLE.**
- **Munby's compilation.** *Inland Transport Statistics, Great Britain 1900-1970* (Clarendon
  Press, 1978) transcribes the railway expenditure series and carries a table with
  "maintenance of rolling stock" split between locomotives, coaching vehicles and wagons. The copy
  in the Internet Archive is lending-restricted; its full text is searchable but the tables cannot
  be read reliably from search snippets. A library copy would give 1920, 1948 and 1966 from one
  source. **UNAVAILABLE (identified, not readable).**
