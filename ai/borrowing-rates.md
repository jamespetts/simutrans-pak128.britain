# Rates at which British companies, and railway companies in particular, could borrow, 1830-2026

**Covers**: published interest rates, coupons and yields at which British companies, and railway
companies in particular, borrowed, 1830 to 2026, with the instrument, term and credit quality of
each figure.

Read with [cost-of-capital](cost-of-capital.md), which specifies the borrowing feature these rates
calibrate.

## Scope, and its limits

This collection is **railway-led**, because for the nineteenth century the railway debenture is
both the best-evidenced and the representative instrument: a large, capital-hungry transport
company borrowing long against secure assets.

It is not sufficient on its own for the twentieth century, for two reasons recorded so that no one
calibrates the feature from it alone:

- **The modern railway rate is the state's, not a company's.** British railways were nationalised
  in 1948, and Network Rail's debt is sovereign-guaranteed and since 2014 borrowed from the
  Department for Transport. There is no private railway market rate to be had after that date.
- **The twentieth-century fleet is mostly not railway.** Buses, coaches, lorries, ships and
  aircraft carry the pakset from the inter-war years onward, and each was financed differently:
  road fleets by bank borrowing, hire purchase and later leasing; ships against a ship mortgage;
  aircraft against the asset, with export credit and sale-and-leaseback.

What the feature needs is the **commercial rate for a private company**, by era and by credit
quality, with asset-secured rates where a vehicle is the security. The railway series is the core
of the nineteenth-century answer, not the whole of the twentieth.

Data appendix: slightly over the usual document length because the seven tables and the shared
assessment are one body of evidence.
Research output for calibrating the planned borrowing feature (1, 2, 5 and 10 year bonds;
instalment loans; credit rating). Every entry records a rate as the source states it.

**Instrument conventions used here**

- **Debenture / debenture stock** — long-term debt secured on the company's assets. In the
  British market these were usually undated (perpetual) stocks with a fixed coupon, traded on
  the Stock Exchange. A **debenture loan** was a secured loan for a fixed term, renewable at
  maturity.
- **Mortgage** — a secured loan on specific property, usually for a stated term.
- **Preference share/stock** — equity in law, fixed dividend, ranking behind debt and ahead of
  ordinary shares. Not secured.
- **Ordinary share/stock** — residual equity; dividend variable and not a contractual payment.
- **Coupon** — the rate the instrument pays on its nominal amount. **Yield** — the return to a
  buyer at a given price. The two are recorded separately and never substituted for each other.
- **Rate paid** — the average rate actually borne by a class of debt, as returned by companies.

All rates are per cent per annum. No rate has been converted, deflated, annualised or adjusted.

## Assessment

**Best long-run series for railway borrowing cost.** The Bank of England M11 railway debenture
yield, 1854-1914 (Table 1), is the only continuous series of the instrument British railways
actually issued. It is monthly, it distinguishes yield from coupon, and it carries a consol
yield in the same sheet, so a railway-over-government spread can be read directly. Its limits
are that it covers "selected" leading companies, that it is a yield rather than a coupon, and
that 1854-1862 is a proxy built from preference stock.

For 1914 onwards the Bank's A31 "Yield on Corporate Bonds" continues the same construction
(railway debentures spliced to industrial debentures), but from 1914 it is no longer a railway
series and must be labelled corporate.

**Range by era, railway debt**

| Era | Secured railway debt | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1840-1865 | 5.0 per cent coupon on mortgages and renewable debentures; 4.10 average rate paid on funded debenture stock and 4.42 on renewable debenture loans in 1865 | F14-F18 |
| 1854-1914 | yield 2.43 to 4.35, typically 3.0-4.1; below 3.0 through most of the 1890s; 4 per cent the standard coupon | F1-F13, F19 |
| 1905 (named companies) | 3.0 and 4.0 coupons on debenture stock | F20-F22 |
| 1923-1936 | 3.0 and 4.0 coupons; yields 3¼ (GWR 4% debentures, 1936) to nearly 4 (LNER 4% guaranteed, 1936) | F23-F25, F69 |
| 1948 | 3.0 coupon at par, Treasury-guaranteed | F26-F27 |
| 1965-1981 | 6.55 to 14.88 long gilt; corporate bond yield 7.07 to 15.94 | F62, BoE A31 |
| 1997 | 7.375 coupon, 7.33 yield, 78 basis points over gilt, 25 years | F28, F66 |
| 2026 | Bank Rate 4.00; no railway market rate exists because Network Rail debt is sovereign-guaranteed | F57 |

**Is a term structure possible?** Only partially.

- Before 1900 there is no series. The one usable observation is qualitative: 5 per cent
  debentures renewable for 3, 5 or 7 years at the lender's option (F63), with no stated rate
  difference by term.
- 1922-1965 gives a workable two-point structure for corporate borrowers: bank advance rate
  (short) against corporate bond yield (long), with the long rate above the short in both years
  examined (F64, F65). This cannot be extended to 1, 2, 5 and 10 years; it is short against
  long only.
- 1997 gives one long point with an explicit gilt spread (F66). A full 1/2/5/10-year corporate
  curve exists only from the late 1990s in commercial index data, which this run did not obtain.

**Principal gaps**

1. **No railway-specific series 1914-1948.** Yields for the Big Four's debentures and guaranteed
   stocks are available only as scattered press quotations (F69). The Bank's corporate series
   from 1914 is industrial.
2. **Coupons are far thinner than yields.** Only two companies (Belfast and County Down;
   Callander and Oban) yielded a full instrument ladder in the 1905 manual consulted, and the
   Board of Trade average for 1865 (F17, F18) is the only whole-sector rate figure found
   before 1900.
3. **No 1830-1853 series.** The debenture yield series starts in 1854; before that there are
   individual mortgage deeds and press reports only.
4. **No published overdraft rate before 1922.** The Bank's advance-rate series starts in 1922.
   Nineteenth-century overdraft pricing must be inferred from bill rates and Bank Rate, which
   is not the same instrument.
5. **No rating-scale evidence.** Historical credit quality is observable only through seniority,
   distress and market spread, not through grades that map to a modern rating scale.
6. **Railway debt after 2014 is not a market credit.** Network Rail's debt carries an
   unconditional UK sovereign guarantee and, since September 2014, the company borrows from the
   Department for Transport rather than the capital markets. Any post-2014 "railway borrowing
   rate" is a government rate, not a corporate one.
7. **The 1896-1897 trough is a legal artefact.** The Trust Investment Acts raised debenture
   prices and compressed the railway-over-consol spread to about 0.2 points, with the railway
   yield at 2.5 per cent. A calibration that reads 1896 as a normal cost of capital will
   understate it.

**What the evidence supports for calibration.** A railway bond rate should be modelled as a
government rate plus a spread. The spreads in this paragraph are computed from the stated yields
in Tables 1 and 7: roughly 0.6-0.8 points in the 1850s-1870s, 0.2-0.3 by the 1890s, 0.25-0.45 in
1900-1914, and about 0.8 in 1997. The coupon on secured railway debt was remarkably stable at
3-4 per cent from the 1860s to 1948, and the variation in borrowing cost came through price
(yield), not through the coupon. Junior capital (preference) cost 0.5-1.0 point more than secured
debt within the same company, and a distressed company paid 10 per cent against 5 in 1848.
