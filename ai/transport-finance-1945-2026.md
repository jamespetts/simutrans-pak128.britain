# Transport finance, 1945-2026

**Covers**: hire purchase and leasing for road fleets, ship mortgages, aircraft finance, and
sector evidence from bus, coach and rail company issues, 1945 to 2026. Parent:
[cost-of-capital](cost-of-capital.md). The general commercial rates are in
[commercial-rates-1945-2026](commercial-rates-1945-2026.md).

## Assessment

### The best long run for commercial borrowing cost

Two Bank of England series, used together, cover 1945 to 2016 without a break and are the best
available long run:

- **Bond market**: S1, the monthly yield on debentures, loan stocks and other corporate bonds,
  1945-2005 (C1-C12), continued by S4, the sterling investment-grade index yield and
  option-adjusted spread, 1997-2016 (C16-C20). Both are yields on traded instruments rather than
  coupons, and both carry a government yield in the same source, so the spread is stated rather
  than computed.
- **Bank borrowing**: S2, the "Rate on Corporate Borrowing", 1922-2016 (C25-C32), and S3, the
  PNFC effective loan rate with its stated spread over 3 month LIBOR, 1977-2016 (C33-C40). The
  first is a rate on advances and is the closest published measure of an overdraft rate; the
  second is the effective rate on all bank loans to companies.

For 2017 to 2026 neither series continues. The only continuous official series found are the
Bank Rate (R5) and the SME new-lending rate from 2016 (C41-C45). Bond-market evidence for
2017-2026 exists only as individual issues (C68, C69, C74) and as fund-manager spread statements
(C22-C24).

### Approximate rates by decade

This table is an assessment, not a set of source figures. Each range is a reading of the rows
cited in the last column. "Good credit" means an established company able to issue a long-dated
bond at or above BBB. "Moderate credit" means a smaller or weaker company borrowing from a bank
or issuing below investment grade. Hire purchase is separate because its flat-rate convention
makes it much more expensive than the quoted number implies.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| 1950-1959 | 4.1 to 5.4 | about 4.5 | 7.5 to 9.0 | C2, C3, C26, C48, C49 |
| 1960-1969 | 6.5 to 7.1 | about 6.9 | 8.5 to 13.0 | C4, C5, C27, C51, C52 |
| 1970-1979 | 10.8 to 19.4 | 10.1 to 18.4 | 16.5 flat, stated as a true rate near 32 | C6, C7, C28, C29, C33, C54 |
| 1980-1989 | 14.2 to 16.2 | 14.3 to 18.4 | Not found | C8, C13, C29, C34, C35 |
| 1990-1999 | 8.3 to 12.2 | 10.3 to 16.7 | Not found | C9, C10, C30, C36, C37 |
| 2000-2009 | 5.2 to 6.1 | 5.2 to 7.7 | Not found | C11, C12, C31, C38, C39 |

The 2020-2026 good-credit cell is the only cell in the table obtained by addition: it is the 10
year gilt par yield at 30 October 2026 (5.2497, R3) plus the investment-grade spreads stated in
C22 to C24 (1.02 to 1.60). The sub-investment-grade airline bonds of 2021 (C68, C69) at 2.75 and
3.75 per cent are lower because they were issued when the reference rate was near zero.

The two columns are not a spread over one common reference. In 2016 a bank loan at about 3 per
cent (C32, C40) was cheaper than a long-dated bond at 2.5 to 4.8 per cent (C14, C15, C20),
because Bank Rate was 0.25 and the bond market priced a term premium. The same inversion appears
in 2026, when the SME new-lending rate of 6.55 (C45) sits close to the gilt-plus-spread
calculation for a good credit.

Three things follow from the table.

1. The gap between good and moderate credit is small in the 1940s and 1950s, when the advance
   rate sat at or below Bank Rate (C26) and the corporate bond spread over gilts was 0.53 to 1.07
   points (C1-C4). It widens from the 1970s. From 1980 the stated spread on bank lending to all
   companies over LIBOR runs from 1.05 to 3.34 points (C34-C40), and the SME-only margin in
   2023-2026 is about 2.2 to 2.8 points over Bank Rate (C43-C45).
2. For most of the period a good credit could borrow long at roughly the gilt yield plus one
   point. The stated spreads in C1-C12 run from 0.53 (1960) to 2.20 (1974) points; the
   investment-grade index spreads in C16-C20 run from 0.61 (1997) to 4.11 (2008) points, the
   extreme occurring only in the 2008 crisis.
3. A moderate credit's cost is driven more by the reference rate than by the spread. In 1980 a
   moderate company paid about 17 per cent mostly because Bank Rate was 14 (C29, C34); in 2021 a
   sub-investment-grade airline paid 3.75 per cent on an eight-year bond (C69) because the
   reference rate was near zero.

### What is known about asset-secured rates for ships and aircraft

**Ships.** The evidence is thin and mostly governmental. The commercial points are:

- 4.25 per cent over 20 years, Shell tanker programme, 1957, from Dutch institutional investors
  (C56). Security is not stated.
- Bank advances against ship mortgage limited to 60 per cent of the bank's own valuation, 1957
  (C57). This is a loan-to-value limit, not a price.
- Typical margins of 0.5 to 2.5 points over 6 month LIBOR for commercial ship mortgages, from a
  survey published in the 2000s (C62). Two to ten year terms, first mortgage plus assignment of
  charter income and insurance.
- Government-guaranteed ship credit at 5.00 per cent (1963), 5.50 per cent (1965-1972) and 7.00
  per cent (1972) for 80 per cent of the price over eight to ten years (C58-C60). The 1969
  statement that 5.50 was "well below current commercial rates" is the only indication of the
  gap, and it gives no number.
- British shipowners were lightly geared: 16 per cent of capital employed in loans in 1969
  (C61). The sector's borrowing rate therefore mattered less to it than to the capital-intensive
  land transport sectors.

**Aircraft.** The evidence is better, because airlines disclose their bonds and aircraft
securitisation publishes tranche coupons.

- Unsecured airline debt spans 10.875 per cent (C63, maturity 2008), 7.25 per cent (C64, 2001,
  with a step-up to 8.75 on downgrade), 5.80 per cent convertible (C65, 2009) and 2.75 and 3.75
  per cent for a B1-rated group in 2021 (C68, C69).
- Secured aircraft debt in the same airline at the same moment was priced in two tranches: 4.25
  per cent for the A-rated senior tranche at 57 per cent loan-to-value and 8.375 per cent for the
  BBB-rated junior tranche at 75 per cent (C66, C67). The airline's issuer rating was BB, so the
  senior secured tranche priced six notches above the airline and the junior three (S17). The
  difference between the two tranches is 4.125 points (**computed**).
- Export credit support is documented for ships (C58-C60) and for the 2021 UKEF-guaranteed
  British Airways loan (C70), but no rate is published for the aircraft facility. This is the
  largest gap in the aircraft evidence.

### Main gaps

1. **No rating-bucket series before December 1997.** The whole 1945-1996 record is a single
   investment-grade aggregate. A credit curve for the pre-1997 period cannot be read from any
   published British source found. Any calibration that gives a 1950s or 1970s company a
   rating-dependent spread is a design choice, not a measured one.
2. **No bus or coach company rates before 1948.** The holding-company structure means the
   operating companies' debentures were not separately reported in the sources consulted.
3. **No shipping company loan capital rates at all.** No P&O, Cunard, Blue Funnel or other
   shipping debenture coupon was found. The shipping figures are the 1957 Shell loan, the
   loan-to-value limit, the government schemes and a secondary range of LIBOR margins.
4. **No hire purchase or lease rate series after 1974.** The 1975-2026 road fleet finance market
   is documented in trade commentary but no continuous published price series was found.
5. **No rate for export credit agency aircraft support.** UKEF guarantees are documented but the
   margin and premium are not published in the sources found.
6. **No 2017-2026 corporate bond yield series from an official source.** The Bank's series stop
   in 2016. The later figures are individual issues and fund-manager statements, not a series.
7. **The 2020 spreads by rating (C21) do not name their index.** They are usable as a shape, not
   as a level, and should not be spliced to C16-C20.
8. **Flat rates and true rates are not interchangeable.** Every hire purchase row before 1974 is
   a flat rate. Treating a 1960 flat rate of 8.5 as an 8.5 per cent cost of capital understates
   the cost by close to half. The source gives the factor for 1974 only (C54).
